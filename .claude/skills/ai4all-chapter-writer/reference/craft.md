# Textbook writing craft — AI for All

## Openings
- Start every chapter and most subsections with something concrete: a scene, a decision, a surprising result. The concept comes second.
- The first 150 words of a chapter must tell the reader why this matters to their studies or job.

## Explaining
- Pattern: example → concept → general rule → non-example → "where this breaks".
- One idea per paragraph. Put the point in the first sentence.
- Analogies are welcome, but each one ends with its limit ("The analogy breaks when…").
- Prefer verbs to nouns ("the model predicts" not "the prediction process of the model").
- Numbers: give a comparison ("about the length of a short novel") alongside the figure.

## Signposting and flow
- Each subsection ends with a one-sentence bridge to the next.
- Use the AI Work Loop (Frame → Prompt → Verify → Decide → Disclose) as the recurring spine; name the stage you are in.
- Summaries restate objectives as abilities ("You can now…").

## Tone
- Calm, practical, neither hype nor doom. The reader is a capable adult from any major.
- Respectful of disciplines outside computing; show what each field contributes.
- Humor is light and never at the reader's expense.

## Avoid machine-sounding prose
Do not use: "delve", "tapestry", "landscape" (figurative), "in today's fast-paced world", "it is important to note that", "navigate the complexities", "unlock the potential", "game-changer", "seamless", "robust" (outside statistics), "In conclusion,". Avoid stacked triplets in every sentence, rhetorical-question openers in series, and heavy em-dash use. Vary sentence length.

## Inclusion
- Rotate fields, names and contexts; avoid stereotypes, including about majority groups.
- Use people-first, bias-free language (APA 7, chapter 5).
- Examples must be workable without paid tools or a strong technical background.
