## Glossary rows

All Chapter 13 terms are new (Deep chapter; defined in the chapter's own text). **Book-wide (editor decision 3): deepfake, synthetic media, voice cloning, social engineering and cheap fake are defined here for Chapters 15, 16, 18 and 19 to copy verbatim.** "Image token" and "audio token" extend, and do not change, Chapter 3's text-only "token." v2 changes (editor decision 2): "deepfake" narrowed to AI-made media (synthetic media) so it no longer overlaps "cheap fake"; "cheap fake" now "without generative AI". One wording for the absent-signal rule everywhere: "a missing signal proves nothing." "Watermark (media)" is sense-labeled beside Chapter 10's "Watermark (text; Deep)", following the calibration precedent. Reused verbatim, not new: token, context window, hallucination, extrinsic hallucination, independent check, primary source, verdict, lateral reading, outside source, false positive (of a detector; the plain-words box adapts it to "something made by a person"), disclosure (of AI use), process evidence.

| Term | Definition | Türkçe |
|---|---|---|
| Modality | A kind of data, such as text, images, audio or video. | Kip (veri türü) |
| Multimodal model | A model that receives or produces more than one kind of data, such as text, images, audio or video. | Çok kipli model |

| Audio token | A short slice of sound compressed into a code that a model can receive or produce like a text token. | Ses belirteci |
| Diffusion model | A generator that starts from random noise and removes it step by step, guided by a prompt, until an image (or audio or video) appears. | Difüzyon modeli |
| Synthetic media | Images, audio or video generated or substantially altered by AI. | Sentetik medya |
| Deepfake | Synthetic media that realistically shows a real person, or a real event, saying, doing or happening in a way that did not happen. | Derin sahte (deepfake) |
| Voice cloning | Using AI to produce new speech that imitates a particular person's voice from samples of it. | Ses klonlama |
| Alt text | A short text alternative for an image that tells a reader who cannot see it what they need to know from it. | Alternatif metin |
| Closed captions | On-screen text of a video's speech and important sounds, which viewers can turn on or off. | Kapalı altyazı |
| Transcription (speech recognition) | Turning spoken audio into written text. | Döküm (konuşma tanıma) |

| Content credentials | A provenance record attached to a file under the C2PA standard: signed statements about how the file was made and edited. | İçerik kimlik bilgileri |

## Chapter 13

### Check Yourself
- **13.1.** (1) A small square patch of an image, turned into numbers that a multimodal model receives the way it receives text tokens (§13.1). (2) Large images are shrunk to a cap before they become patches, so fine detail such as small numbers can disappear (§13.1, dated box). (3) Metadata such as the date, location or camera (one provider also names file names); the provider still receives the file with that information (§13.1).
- **13.2.** (1) Training teaches the model to predict and remove noise from captioned images; to generate, it starts from random noise and removes a little at each of many steps, steered by the prompt, until an image appears (§13.2). (2) A deepfake is synthetic media that realistically shows a real person, or a real event, saying, doing or happening in a way that did not happen; voice cloning is using AI to produce new speech that imitates a particular person's voice from samples of it (§13.2). (3) A generator produces what is plausible for a prompt, not light recorded from an event, so a realistic picture can record nothing (§13.2, F13.6).
- **13.3.** (1) A short text alternative that tells a reader who cannot see an image what they need to know from it (§13.3). (2) 38% (§13.3). (3) The reader who relies on it cannot see the image, so cannot compare the text with it (§13.3).
- **13.4.** (1) Provenance records where a file came from and how it was changed, as stated by whoever signed it; it cannot say whether the content is true (§13.4). (2) Credentials are often stripped when files are shared, and many files never had one, so a missing signal proves nothing (§13.4). (3) A hash, a fingerprint computed from the file's contents, so that a change shows (§13.4).
- **13.5.** (1) A pattern, visible or hidden, that a tool places in the media it produces, which a matching detector can later test for (§13.5). (2) Any two: the maker did not add one; an open-weight model run by anyone added none; editing weakened or removed it (§13.5). (3) A signal that is present and valid is a lead about where content came from; a missing signal proves nothing (§13.5).
- **13.6.** (1) Accuracy rose from 48.2% to 59.0%, about 11 percentage points (§13.6). (2) It fell from 0.87–1.00 on the original test data to 0.43–0.63, close to guessing (0.5) (§13.6). (3) It labels a person's real work as AI-made, which can damage that person's reputation or treatment (§13.6).
- **13.7.** (1) Confirming a request or claim through a separate channel you already trust, not one the message provides; for example, calling the number in the company directory (§13.7). (2) Media manipulated without generative AI, such as a real photo with a false caption or date; checking the earliest version and outside sources shows the true context (§13.7). (3) Clues are weak: they can confirm what the context showed, but rarely settle a case alone (§13.7, §13.6).
- **13.8.** (1) The moment of pressure is when people skip checks; deciding in advance means you only have to follow the rule (§13.8). (2) Numbers change, and a written list of real numbers can be copied or faked; a role points you to the current, trusted listing (§13.8). (3) Part 6, disclosure (§13.8).

### Faded examples
- **13.1, anatomy poster:** at risk: the small labels near the edges, because the 4,000-pixel photo is shrunk to the tool's cap before it becomes image tokens. Check: compare every listed bone with the poster itself, or photograph the poster in sections.
- **13.2, product mockup:** the sky, sand, sunset, light and shadows record nothing; only the bottle records the real product. The post should say the scene was generated (Chapter 10, disclosure), so no one reads it as a photo of the product in use.
- **13.3, lecture captions:** check 2: "0.05" (not "0.5" or "five"), "30" (not "13"), and the symbols around them ("p-value below"); harmed if wrong: viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing and anyone studying from the captions.
- **13.4, three photos:** *Photo B:* proves nothing either way; the credential may have been stripped or never added; check the source and the earliest version. *Photo C:* the AI tool's own record says it generated or edited the image; it cannot run as a news photo of the event.
- **13.5, labeled renders:** the label does not show what the product will look like, who made the render, or that unlabeled images are real. If the corner is cropped, the visible label is gone; a hidden watermark may or may not survive.
- **13.6, vendor claim:** without a false-positive rate, you do not know how often real interviews would be flagged; test the tool on interviews whose origin you know before using it, or do not use it to exclude anyone. A false positive could get a real participant's interview excluded or the participant suspected of fraud.
- **13.7, accounts-payable voice message:** the request is put on hold: no change made and no reply in the same channel until the controller confirms. A written rule: every bank-detail change is confirmed by calling a known number from the directory, and approved by a second person.
- **13.8, social-media manager:** pause: no repost for 30 minutes, and none until an original source is found. High-stakes checks: earliest version, two outside sources (officials, established local news), signals; if unsettled, do not post. Disclosure: label any image the club generated or edited ("edited image," "AI-generated illustration") in the post itself.

### Review Questions A
1. A model that receives or produces more than one kind of data, such as text, images, audio or video. Modalities: any two of text, images, audio, video.
3. Training teaches the model to predict and remove noise from captioned images. To generate, it starts from random noise and removes a little at each of many steps, with the prompt steering each step, until an image appears (often in a compressed latent version, decoded at the end).
5. Memorization means a generator can reproduce an image it was trained on, mostly one that appeared many times in the training data (Carlini et al., 2023). Even then, the output reproduces a picture; it does not record an event happening now, and a new generated image records no event at all. Whether an image records an event depends on where it came from, which section 13.7 checks.
7. The rate is small but not zero, and the reader of a filed transcript usually does not have the audio, so cannot spot an invented sentence. The person who files the transcript must check it against the audio, especially where invented content would harm someone (38% of transcriptions with inventions contained harmful content in the study).
9. A signal that is present and valid is a lead about where content came from; a missing signal proves nothing. Reasons for no watermark: the maker did not add one (for example, an open-weight model run by anyone); or editing weakened or removed it. (A watermark may also be present but unfound, if you lack the maker's detector.)
11. On media flagged in 2024, nine open-source detectors scored an AUC of 0.43–0.63, near guessing (0.5), although they had scored 0.87–1.00 on their original test data; even the best commercial tools stayed below 90% accuracy (Chandra et al., 2025). A score that changes this much with the kind of media cannot settle one case; it can only point you to check the source and context.

### Review Questions B (model answers)
1. *Received:* a grid of image tokens of the photo, shrunk first if it is large, and the prompt. *Not received:* the file's metadata (date, camera, location), the painting itself, the museum's records. *Checks:* (1) every visual claim against the photo and, if possible, the painting (colors, objects, number of figures); (2) every factual claim (artist, date, title, medium) against the museum's catalog record, a primary source. Credit for naming the resizing risk for small details and for checking facts against records, not the tool.
3. *AI-tool credential:* a lead that the image was generated or edited with that tool; it cannot run as a news photo of the storm. *No credential:* proves nothing either way. Next: source and channel (who sent it, can the original be had?), earliest version (reverse search), outside sources (weather service, local news, other photos). Credit for the two-sided rule and an ordered set of context checks.

### Review Questions C (scoring guidance)
1. Strong answers reject the absolute claim with the chapter's evidence: generated images record no event (13.2), so realism is not evidence; but photographs with provenance (a valid credential, an original file from a known person) and context checks (earliest version, outside sources) can still support claims (13.4, 13.7), as the archive photo in 13.7 shows; the liar's dividend shows the cost of dismissing all photos. Positions that keep photographs as evidence "with provenance and corroboration" or that restrict them in specific settings can both earn full marks if argued from the chapter. Weak answers rely on looking for flaws (M3) or on detectors (M4).

### Spaced Review
1. Automation bias is the tendency to over-rely on automated output, following it when it is wrong or missing what it did not flag (Chapter 11). A "92% likely real" score can lead someone to share the clip without asking where it came from, although detectors fell toward guessing on recent media and the missing source is the stronger warning.
3. Detectors and judges can label hand-made work as AI-made (false positives), and the harm falls on the human maker; Hana's collective answers with a process-evidence rule for everyone rather than suspicion of some.
5. Whether the tool's terms allow this input: are uploads used for training, who can see them, and how long are they kept; and whether the input's class allows it at all: a recording of other people's voices is personal data, so the group's consent and a tool with institutional terms (or no upload) are needed (Chapter 12; Chapter 15).
