### Review Questions B
1. Chat assistant (institutional terms): receives what staff paste; produces text; provider's service. Document editor's AI feature (productivity suite): receives open documents; drafts and summarizes; institutional terms. 3. The email addresses are personal data about other people: no consumer tool. Lock-in: prompts and examples kept only inside the tool, chat history, standing instructions, habits. Next time: prompts and test inputs in the owner's own files, and a re-test trigger for model retirements.

### Review Questions C
1. Open. Strong answers argue for one licensed tool plus a fit matrix by role, or against one tool for everything: fit belongs to a part and a tool together (BCG: the same tool helped on 18 tasks and hurt on one), data classes differ by role, and people whose tasks sit outside the tool's frontier bear the cost. Strong answers also grant the benefits of one license (institutional terms, training, cost) and propose bake-offs per role and re-test triggers. Weak answers name "the best tool" without evidence.
3. Strong answers disagree: in the 2025 study, developers forecast 24% faster and felt 20% faster, but their tasks took 19% longer; time must be measured, including checking. Strong answers note the study's limits (16 experienced developers on familiar projects, early-2025 tools) and METR's 2026 update (a new round pointed toward a speedup but was judged unreliable), so the point is measurement, not that tools always slow people down.

### Spaced Review
1. Equal weights assume every criterion matters equally: a judgment, not a fact (Chapter 11). A sensitivity check changes one choice, such as one weight, and sees whether the result changes. In a fit matrix the gate is a veto and each part gets its own decision, so one total across parts hides the decisions that matter.
3. Chapter 10's three questions: does a rule require disclosure (the club's or the sponsors' expectations)? do these readers expect his own words (a thank-you from the captain: probably yes for the personal parts)? would a reasonable reader feel misled later? If any answer is yes, disclose, for example with a short line or a note to the club's advisor; he answers for the message either way.

### Case Study Questions
1. The spreadsheet looked complete, but the interview notes held details that pointed to a different answer; the authors expected AI, prompted with the instructions, the data and the interviews, to reach the contrasting (wrong) conclusion. Polished output made it easy to accept.
3. Tree: hallucination, either leaf with a reason ("contradicts its input or source": the interview notes held the decisive details; or "wrong calculation or reasoning": the authors say the task was designed so GPT-4 would err in its analysis), inferred (dashed), because the paper does not report the outputs each consultant saw. Chain: links 2–3 (not checked; relied on), documented by the authors' statement that those who did worse questioned the output less (automation bias, Chapter 11); link 4: an experiment, no real-world harm.
5. Open. A wrong answer that looks grounded is usually costlier for a law firm, because it can reach a filing (Chapter 8's *Mata* case); an incomplete answer costs time but is visible. A bake-off with the firm's own typical, different and edge questions, scored for correctness (with an independent check of each cited source), completeness and time, would show each tool's failure type.
