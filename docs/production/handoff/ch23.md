### Check Yourself
**23.1** (1) A stated premise is written in the argument; an unstated one is assumed but not written. A map draws an unstated premise with a dashed outline, because it is the reader's reconstruction (inferred) (§23.1). (2) It shows exactly where the two sides part ways, so the disagreement becomes smaller and clearer (§23.1). (3) The strongest fair version of a view, one its holders would accept; without it, a debate is won against a straw man (§23.1).
**23.2** (1) He proposed the imitation game in its place and treated sustained, satisfactory answers in the game as the evidence to use; he did not claim that the machines of his day think (§23.2). (2) (1) A program manipulates symbols by their form (syntax); (2) form alone is not enough for meaning (semantics); (3) the person in the room follows the rules and understands no Chinese. The robot reply accepts premise 2 and adds connections to the world: a program linked to cameras and motors might understand. Searle answered that the camera signals would reach the person as more symbols (§23.2). (3) It raised the share of games in which the models were judged human from 36–38% to 56–73%. The authors describe the test as a measure of how people tell humans from machines; Turing proposed such behavior as the evidence to use, and Searle argued that it cannot settle understanding (§23.2).
**23.3** (1) Phenomenal consciousness is experience, something it is like to be the system; moral status is having interests that matter morally for their own sake. They are linked but distinct (§23.3). (2) A self-report is a claim to check, accepted by both sides; Butlin et al. check a system's design against indicator properties derived from scientific theories (§23.3). (3) Each has a stake in the answer: Suleyman leads Microsoft AI, the AI division of a company that sells AI products; Anthropic sells AI products and acted on its welfare uncertainty (§23.3).
**23.4** (1) The practice rule assigns duties in coursework or a workplace; it does not settle whether blame is always just when no one could foresee what a system would do (§23.4). (2) Premise 1: holding someone responsible requires control and foresight. Premise 2: learning systems act in ways their makers and operators cannot predict. Conclusion: a responsibility gap opens; society must accept it or stop using such systems (§23.4). (3) The person nearest an automated system absorbing blame for a failure they had little control over (Elish, 2019) (§23.4).
**23.5** (1) New, surprising and valuable (Boden, 2004) (§23.5). (2) Neither automatically: "creative" is about the output; authorship asks whose choices shaped it; ownership asks who can explain, defend and stand behind it; legal authorship is a separate question in Ch. 18 (§23.5).
