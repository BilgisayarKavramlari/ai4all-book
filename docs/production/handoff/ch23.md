### Check Yourself
**23.1** (1) A stated premise is written in the argument; an unstated one is assumed but not written. A map draws an unstated premise with a dashed outline, because it is the reader's reconstruction (inferred) (§23.1). (2) It shows exactly where the two sides part ways, so the disagreement becomes smaller and clearer (§23.1). (3) The strongest fair version of a view, one its holders would accept; without it, a debate is won against a straw man (§23.1).
**23.2** (1) He proposed the imitation game in its place; he did not claim that machines think or that passing proves understanding (§23.2). (2) A program manipulates symbols by their form (syntax); form alone is not enough for meaning (semantics); the person in the room follows the rules and understands no Chinese. The robot reply challenges the second (§23.2). (3) It raised the share of games in which the models were judged human from 36–38% to 56–73%; the test measures how people judge a conversation (style and social cues), not understanding (§23.2).
**23.3** (1) Phenomenal consciousness is experience, something it is like to be the system; moral status is having interests that matter morally for their own sake. They are linked but distinct (§23.3). (2) A self-report is a claim to check, accepted by both sides; Butlin et al. check a system's design against indicator properties derived from scientific theories (§23.3). (3) Each leads or is a company that sells AI products, so each has a stake in the answer (§23.3).
**23.4** (1) The rule assigns duties in a course or workplace; it does not settle whether blame is always just when no one could foresee what a system would do (§23.4). (2) Premise 1: holding someone responsible requires control and foresight. Premise 2: learning systems act in ways their makers and operators cannot predict. Conclusion: a responsibility gap opens; society must accept it or forgo such systems (§23.4). (3) The person nearest an automated system absorbing blame for a failure they had little control over (Elish, 2019) (§23.4).
**23.5** (1) New, surprising and valuable (Boden, 2004) (§23.5). (2) Neither automatically: "creative" is about the output; authorship asks whose choices shaped it; ownership asks who can explain, defend and stand behind it; legal authorship is a separate question in Ch. 18 (§23.5).
**23.6** (1) Meaning at work is at stake and depends on how tasks are redesigned and who decides (Bankins & Formosa, 2023); less work could be welcomed if gains are shared (Keynes, 1930; Danaher, 2019) (§23.6). **23.8** (1) The question, a map, side A, side B, the hardest objection and reply, what would change your view, an AI Use Declaration; your view appears only in its own labeled paragraph (§23.8).
