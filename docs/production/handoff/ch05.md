## Glossary rows

New Key Terms (Chapter 5 is the defining chapter; Chapter 6 reuses "in-context example" and "test inputs" word for word):

| Term | Definition | Türkçe |
|---|---|---|
| Task frame | A short statement of a task's goal, audience, success criteria, limits and what stays with you, written before the first prompt. | Görev çerçevesi |
| Success criteria | Checks, written in advance, that an output must pass to count as good; each can be scored met, partly met or not met. | Başarı ölçütleri |
| Delegation Ladder | The book's arrangement of three ways of working with AI (automation, augmentation, agency) over a floor of no AI, ordered by how hard it is to see and check what the AI contributed before it takes effect. | Devretme merdiveni |
| Automation (mode of working with AI) | Working with AI in which the AI carries out a task you define, and you check the output. | Otomasyon |
| Augmentation | Working with AI in which you and the AI shape and carry out the task together, turn by turn. | Güçlendirme (birlikte çalışma) |
| Agency (mode of working with AI) | Working with AI in which you configure it to carry out future tasks on your behalf, often for other people. Not the same as an agent. | Vekâlet kipi (ajanlık) |
| Anatomy of a prompt | The book's six-part description of what a prompt can contain: role, task, context, constraints, in-context examples and output format. | İstem anatomisi |
| Role (prompt part) | The voice, perspective or audience the output should be written for. | Rol (istem bileşeni) |
| Task (prompt part) | What you want done: an action and what it acts on. The one part every prompt needs. | Görev (istem bileşeni) |
| Context (prompt part) | The background a task needs that the tool cannot otherwise receive, such as figures, readers or source text. | Bağlam (istem bileşeni) |
| Constraint | A limit or a must-not in a prompt, such as a length, a source rule or an excluded topic. | Kısıt |
| In-context example | A sample input with the output you want, placed in a prompt to show the pattern. | Bağlam içi örnek |
| Output format | The shape the output should take, such as a table, a list or a word limit, chosen so it can be checked. | Çıktı biçimi |
| Iteration | Improving a prompt by changing one thing at a time, predicting the effect, rerunning and scoring the result against your success criteria. | Yineleme |
| Iteration log | A record of each prompt version: what changed, the prediction, the results of each run and the decision to keep or revert. | Yineleme günlüğü |
| Test inputs | A small set of different inputs a prompt is tried on (typical, different, edge) to see whether it works beyond one case. | Test girdileri |
| Peer swap test | Someone else runs your prompt without your explanation and scores the output against your criteria, to reveal what was in your head but not in the prompt. | Akran takas testi |

## Chapter 5

### Review Questions A
1. Goal, audience, success criteria, limits, and what stays with you (the A3 frame card adds a rung for each part).
3. *Automation:* you define a task, the AI carries it out, and you check the output. *Augmentation:* you and the AI shape and carry out the task together, turn by turn. *Agency:* you configure AI to carry out future tasks on your behalf, often for other people.
5. Agency is a way of working: you set AI up to act on future tasks for you. An agent is a kind of system in which the model directs its own steps and tool use. Example of agency that is not an agent: a course chatbot that answers students' questions from fixed instructions and a fixed source, without choosing tools or steps.
7. **(b)** In a test of four model families on 2,410 factual questions, adding a persona did not improve accuracy overall (Zheng et al., 2024); a role mainly shapes voice and audience. (a) and (d) are M1 (magic words); (c) overstates the finding.
9. **(b)** Each part should serve the task; irrelevant material can lower quality (Shi et al., 2023). (a) is M4; (c) and (d) are not supported.
11. **(c)** For GPT-4o the two wordings made no meaningful overall difference, though single questions swung both ways (Meincke et al., 2025a). (a) and (b) are M1; (d) overstates: formatting changes can matter a lot (Sclar et al., 2024).
13. What was in your head but not in the prompt: context, an audience or a meaning you take for granted, because you always read your own prompt with that knowledge.

### Review Questions B (model answers)
1. *Goal:* a one-page study sheet for an exam on heart medications. *Audience:* the student herself, a junior nursing student. *Criteria:* (1) every drug class and example matches the course textbook; (2) every dose or warning is copied from the textbook or a named reference, not generated; (3) one page, in a table (class, example, main action, key nursing check). *Safety criterion:* no patient information is used, and nothing is added that is not in the sources. *Limit:* only the assigned chapters. *Stays with her:* choosing what the exam covers, checking every drug fact against the textbook, and learning the material herself (consider augmentation in a tutor style, Chapter 7).
3. *Missing parts:* context (the internship's real facts), constraints (length; only facts given; no awards or numbers not supplied), output format (length, tone for LinkedIn), role/audience (recruiters in her field). *Why-chain:* the post mentions an award → why? the prompt gave no facts about the internship, so the output filled in plausible ones → why? she expected the tool to know her experience (in her head, not in the prompt). *v2 (one change):* add context: three real accomplishments and the team's name. *Prediction:* the post uses only those facts; the invented award disappears. (A constraint "do not add facts I did not give" is a good v3.)

### Review Questions C (scoring guidance)
1. Strong answers separate "worked by the measures chosen" from "worked for customers": the 2024 measures were volume, speed, cost and a satisfaction score (Klarna, 2024); in 2025 the chief executive said cost had weighed too heavily and quality had suffered (reported second-hand). Using the chapter: success criteria decide what "worked" means, and moving the human check from before the answer to an option after it changes what must be measured. Accept "agree" if it argues that the company's own data showed success and that the change was strategic, provided it notes that all figures are company claims.
3. Strong answers give reasons tied to the chapter: criteria set by the instructor protect learning goals (the skill-building question in 5.2); criteria set by the student build judgment and fit the student's task; both together is common. Look for checkable criteria in the examples given and for attention to what stays with the student.

### Spaced Review
1. At each step a token is picked with some chance (sampling), so two runs can follow different paths. Step 5 of the iteration loop (rerun at least twice) allows for this.
3. A correction made in a conversation stays in that conversation's context; it does not change the model's settings. A fresh chat does not contain it, so the original prompt can produce the original error again (Chapter 3).
5. Frame, Prompt, Verify, Decide, Disclose. This chapter strengthened Frame and Prompt.

### Case Study Questions
1. *Conversations handled (2.3 million; two-thirds of chats):* volume, not whether problems were solved. *Work of 700 full-time agents:* the company's equivalence, not a measured outcome for customers. *Resolution time (under 2 minutes instead of 11):* speed, not correctness. *Satisfaction on par:* a company-reported score; it does not show who did not answer or who gave up. *25% fewer repeat inquiries:* a useful quality signal, but company-reported and without a method. *$40 million profit improvement:* a forecast of cost, not service quality.
3. *Company claims:* every 2024 figure; the November 2025 figures (853 agents, about $60 million, satisfaction on par). *Opinion:* the Forrester analyst's view that Klarna over-pivoted to cost. *Second-hand:* the May 2025 statements (Bloomberg, reported by Entrepreneur). *Could be checked independently:* customer complaints filed with regulators, independent customer surveys, a published audit of sampled conversations. Evidence that would change the view: an independent sample of conversations scored for whether problems were solved.
5. Example: quality suffered → why? the system was judged mostly on cost (checked: the CEO's own statement, second-hand) → why? the announced measures were volume, speed and cost (checked: press release) → why? quality criteria were not written next to them, or not published (assumed: the record does not show internal criteria).
