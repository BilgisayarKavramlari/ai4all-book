## Chapter 8

### Review Questions A
1. A claim is one statement in an output that you might rely on. Example of one not checkable as true or false: a recommendation such as "You should start with the hardest section," or a prediction.
3. Independent check, primary source, expert.
5. **(b)** Checks are proportional to stakes; every claim you rely on gets at least the independent check. (a) is M6; (c) overcorrects (every claim you rely on gets stage 1); (d) ignores numbers, dates and quotations.
7. **(b)** The citation may point to another work or not exist; match every detail to the record before giving a verdict. (a) treats a real name as proof; (c) is M3; (d) is M2.
9. **(b)** The answer comes from the same kind of process that produced the source, not from evidence outside it. In Mata the replies said the cases were real (ECF 54, ¶ 45). (a) and (c) are false; (d) is irrelevant.
11. Nothing either way. Holding firm is no evidence: in Mata the replies held firm and were wrong. A flip would have been a warning sign; a firm answer still needs a check outside the tool.
13. Calibration of a model: how well a model's stated confidence matches its accuracy. Calibration of your confidence: how well your predicted score matches your actual score (Chapter 7). Two meanings, one habit: predict, then check.

### Review Questions B
1. Claims: (1) Gen Z spends an average of 7.2 hours a day on social media (number; checkable; high stakes); (2) the figure comes from a 2024 Pew Research Center report (citation; checkable; high: go to Pew's own report, the primary source, and find the figure); (3) brands should move all their budget to TikTok (recommendation; not checkable as true or false; it does not follow from claim 1 and needs evidence about returns, not usage). Rank 2 and 1 highest, because a client presentation will repeat them; flag 3 as a judgment. Full credit: three claims, correct marks, a ranking with reasons. (The answer key does not say whether Pew reported this figure; students must check.)
3. Checks in order: does it exist (library catalog or publisher's site), does every detail match (author, title, publisher, year), does page 214 contain the quotation. (a) The book exists and matches but the page does not contain the quotation: unsupported for the quotation (contradicted if the page says the opposite); failure 3. (b) A book of that title exists by a different author: the citation points to another work's details, so it is contradicted as cited (failure 2); check whether the real book contains the quotation. (c) The book appears in no catalog: unsupported, with the catalogs searched named in the uncertainty line; it may be fabricated; do not cite it.

### Review Questions C
1. Open. Strong answers take a clear position, use the Ethics Lens (checking is cheap for the writer and expensive for those downstream) and the stopping rule (stakes decide the stage), and give one example, such as a citation in a court filing or a dose in a patient leaflet, where the reader bears the cost and the writer's check must go further. Weak answers say "always check everything" or "only check when you have time."
3. Open. Strong answers use the stopping rule: low-stakes claims stop at stage 1, so checking need not take longer than writing; high-stakes claims need the primary source either way, because writing it yourself would also require it. A strong answer estimates the time for a real task in the student's field and names which claims drive the cost. Weak answers ignore stakes.

### Spaced Review
1. Hallucination, leaf "invented facts or sources." Usually extrinsic: the user's input can neither support nor contradict it; it would be intrinsic only if it contradicted a source list the user supplied.
3. In A4 you calibrated your own confidence: your predicted closed-book score against your actual score. In A5 you calibrate both your own confidence and the tool's stated confidence against the verdicts. The habit is the same: predict, then check.

### Case Study Questions
1. Hallucination, leaf "invented facts or sources," documented: the record shows the six decisions do not exist and were generated by ChatGPT (¶ 36). Overconfidence, leaf "certainty beyond the evidence," documented: asked directly whether the cases were real, the replies said they were (¶ 45).
3. Stage 1, the independent check: the lawyer's own search on a free case-law site found nothing (¶ 12), which makes the verdict unsupported, and a citation in a court filing cannot rest on an unsupported verdict. Stage 2 would have made it contradicted (the citation and docket belong to other cases, ¶ 28).
5. Mata: *Verify* (open every citation in a legal research database before filing) and *Decide* (the lawyer who signs reads the authorities). Reading list: a stage-1 catalog search for each title and an editorial review before publication; the newspaper said the insert ran without review by its editors. They differ because in Mata a check was done, gave a warning and was ignored, then the lawyers defended the cases; in the reading list, the workflow had no review step at the point of publication.
