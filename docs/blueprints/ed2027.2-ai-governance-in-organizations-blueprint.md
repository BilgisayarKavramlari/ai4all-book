## The gap this chapter fills

Chapter 21 asks a student to write a **personal** code: commitments one person keeps, inside the rules that bind them (section 21.3). The first thing a student in a part-time job, internship or first job meets is not their own code but **someone else's policy**, or a request to help write one, or to "look into" an AI tool a manager wants to buy. Nothing in edition 2027.1 teaches how an organization's AI rules are built, why many are ignored, what to ask a vendor, how an organization keeps track of the AI it runs, or whom to tell when something goes wrong. **The unit of analysis in this chapter is the organization** (a company, a public office, a newsroom, a research lab, a nonprofit, a club), seen by a newcomer who is not a manager.

**The job left for this chapter.** (1) Show what an organizational AI policy contains and which features make a policy hold in practice; (2) explain the gap between policy and practice (shadow AI) and what a newcomer can do about it; (3) give a question list for evaluating an AI tool and its vendor before adoption, with AI-specific questions and the contract's data terms at its center; (4) teach the organization's two records, the AI inventory and the organization-level risk register, built on Chapter 15's register; (5) give an incident path (contain, tell the owner promptly, keep a record, fix together); (6) say what the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 are for, at the level of purpose, not clause.

## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question (answered in the Summary):** *When you join an organization that uses AI, how do you tell whether its rules work, what should it ask before it adopts a tool, and what do you do when something goes wrong?* Answer (hedged): read the policy for its owner, its sanctioned path, its checks and its review date; ask the vendor what the AI is built on, what testing shows, and what the contract (not the brochure) says about data, security, changes and exit; expect an inventory and a register, and add to them; when something goes wrong, contain it if you safely can, tell the named owner promptly and keep a record as you go; the frameworks give the organization a shared map, and none of them replaces the law (Ch. 18).

**Opening Case (<= 400 words, aim 300): CNET's AI-assisted explainers (2022-2023).** One job: facts and puzzle. Confirmed from the publisher's own note (Guglielmo, January 25, 2023): from November (2022), one editorial team used an internally designed AI engine to help create short financial explainers; 77 stories, about 1% of the site's content in that period; after one story was cited for factual errors the team audited all of them, corrected several (a small number substantially), paused the tool, changed the byline and made the disclosure more visible; a plagiarism checker was not properly used or missed close matches. Reported: outside writers noticed the AI use before readers were told clearly (Futurism, around January 11-12, 2023 [VERIFY exact date in the original article]; CNN, January 25, 2023, headline and date confirmed, body via search summary only). **Puzzle:** *Who inside the organization should have known what the tool was doing, and what records would have let it find the errors before a reader did?* Answered in G.4-G.5 and the Summary. **Label:** hallucination (wrong facts in published output; documented by the publisher's correction notes) on the Failure Taxonomy Tree, plus chain link "no check before publication" (inferred from the note's audit account) [the editor confirms the label against the Ch. 4 rules].

(Verify, optional, 25 min; O3).** A vendor fact sheet and a two-page constructed contract excerpt (model provider named or not, training clause, retention, subprocessor list, breach-notice clause, change notice, exit): answer the ten questions and mark where each answer is (contract / marketing / nowhere).
