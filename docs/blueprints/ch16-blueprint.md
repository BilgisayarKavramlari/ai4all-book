## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question, answered in the Summary:** *How can you tell whether an AI system treats different groups of people fairly, and who gets to decide what fair means?* Answer (hedged): look for bias at four places — the data, the model, the way the system is used, and the feedback that turns its decisions into new data. Measure group by group, including groups defined by more than one characteristic, and report how many cases each group had: an overall number can hide large gaps, and small samples produce gaps by chance. "Fair" can mean equal selection rates, equal error rates or equal precision; when groups differ in how often the outcome really occurs, a tool generally cannot have all three, so someone must choose which comparison matters most for this decision. That choice is not only technical: the people who bear the errors should have a say, and the decision owner answers for it. Mitigation helps when it is aimed at the phase where the bias entered, and it can hide a problem or move it somewhere else, so test again after every fix.

| 16.3 | What "fair" can mean, and why the meanings collide (≤ 550) | Core | **Three fairness definitions answer three different questions, and when groups differ in how often the outcome occurs, a tool generally cannot satisfy all three at once.** **Fairness definition** (new Core term): a stated rule for which comparison between groups must come out equal. For a tool that flags people (Elif's outreach score): **equal selection rates** — is the same share of each group flagged? (also called demographic parity); **equal error rates** — among people who needed help, is the same share **missed** in each group (**miss rate**, = 1 − recall, Ch. 2), and among people who did not, is the same share flagged anyway (**false-positive rate**, new sense-labeled term generalizing Ch. 10's detector sense)? (Hardt et al., 2016, call equal miss rates "equal opportunity"); **equal precision** — does a flag mean the same thing in each group: of the flagged, the same share really needed help? (precision, Ch. 2). **Base rate** (new Core term): how often the outcome actually occurs in a group. **The computed demo** (this book's, exact counts, no randomness; figures/scripts/ch16_experiments.py → data/ch16_tradeoff.csv; constructed groups A and B of 1,000 students each; base rates 50% and 30%; the score is built to mean the same in both groups): with **one threshold** (flag at 0.5), precision is almost equal (65.4% vs. 66.0%), but group A's students who did not need help are flagged far more often (false-positive rate 45.0% vs. 12.1%) and group B's students who needed help are missed far more often (miss rate 15.0% vs. 45.0%); selection rates 65% vs. 25%. ## 12. Risks and checks

- **Gender classification is not face recognition.** Gender Shades tested software that labels perceived gender, not software that identifies people. Every mention says "gender classification" (glossed once) and keeps identification claims for NIST (Grother et al., 2019) and Case short 2. The paper's own first paragraph speculates about identification; the book does not borrow that as a finding.
- **Group labels as the studies define them.** "Darker-skinned women" = Fitzpatrick IV–VI and perceived female, as labeled for PPB; the book says "as the authors labeled skin type and perceived gender" once and states the binary-label limitation. Bianchi et al.'s race categories come from an automated classifier; said in the figure source line and the text.
- **Wilson & Caliskan correction (Aug 2026).** Gender-only results were inverted by a code error (authors' note on arXiv v3, Aug 30, 2026); only the race and intersectional results are used, and the correction is stated.
