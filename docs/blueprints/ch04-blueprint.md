## 4. Misconceptions (refutation pattern; six)

| # | Misconception | Where | Refutation, without creating a new one |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | "Hallucinations are rare glitches that the next update will remove" | 4.2 | They follow from producing likely text and from scoring that rewards a guess over "I don't know" (Kalai et al., 2025). Newer models, retrieval and tools reduce them on many tasks; as of 2026 none removes them. Avoid "never" and "always" |
| M2 | "If the tool says it is sure, or agrees when I push back, it is probably right" | 4.4 | Stated confidence and agreement can follow training pressures rather than evidence (OpenAI, 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). Not "confidence is meaningless": it is a weak signal that you still check |
| M3 | "Once I have given the tool a piece of information, it will use it for the rest of the conversation" | 4.3 | Long conversations lose constraints; early assumptions persist (Laban et al., 2025). Distinct from Ch. 3 M5 (one long input) |
| M4 | "A polite, neutral-sounding answer cannot be biased; bias is a problem of hiring and face-recognition systems" | 4.5 | Generated text inherits patterns from its training data and feedback; covert dialect bias in 2024 models (Hofmann et al., 2024) |
| M5 | "A document or web page I give the tool is only data; it cannot change what the tool does" | 4.6 | Instructions and data arrive in the same token stream (Ch. 3), so text inside a document can act as an instruction (Greshake et al., 2023) |
| M6 | "When an AI tool makes the mistake, the tool, not the company or person using it, is responsible" | 4.7 | The tribunal called the airline's "separate legal entity" argument remarkable and held the airline responsible for all information on its website (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024, paras. 27–28). Scope: one small-claims decision in British Columbia, not a general rule of law (Ch. 18) |

"The model lies" (anthropomorphism) is handled by the wording rule and one sentence in 4.2 ("there is no intent to deceive; the system produces likely text"), not as a seventh misconception.

## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question, answered in the Summary:** *Is AI failure a bug that will be fixed, or a feature of how these systems work?* Answer (hedged): each branch of the tree follows from how the systems are built and used, so failures are expected, not exceptional; better models, retrieval and tools lower some rates on some tasks; whether an error becomes a harm depends on the people and organizations around the tool.

**Opening Case (450–600 words): *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149.** Facts only from the decision (verified 2026-10-18): on November 11, 2022, after their grandmother died, Jake Moffatt asked a support chatbot on Air Canada's website about bereavement fares (para. 13–15). The chatbot's reply said they could submit a ticket for a reduced bereavement rate within 90 days of the ticket's issue date, including after travel (para. 15, paraphrased). The words "bereavement fares" in that reply linked to the airline's own policy page, which said the policy does not apply after travel is completed (paras. 16–17). Moffatt booked flights on November 11 and 16 (para. 18), applied on November 17 (para. 20), and in February 2023 an airline representative admitted the chatbot had used "misleading words" (para. 22). Air Canada argued that it could not be held liable for the chatbot (paras. 4, 27). On February 14, 2024, Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers found negligent misrepresentation and ordered the airline to pay $812.02 in total: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in pre-judgment interest and $125 in tribunal fees (paras. 32, 40–44). Note for the text: Air Canada gave no information about the nature of its chatbot (para. 14); the decision does not say it was generative AI. The case is told with the decision's own pronouns for Moffatt (they/them) and amounts in Canadian dollars.
**Chapter question:** "The chatbot was wrong and the airline's own page was right. Why would a chatbot say that, and who answers for it?" The tribunal answered the second half; Core 4.2, 4.4 and 4.7 answer the first as far as the evidence allows, and mark the rest as inference.

**Prediction before the evidence (4.2):** "A model trained only on true sentences: can it still produce false ones?" Answered by the computed toy in F4.4.

**How the tree is built.** Signature 5 is drawn in full (no causes) in 4.1, then each of 4.2–4.6 shows the same tree with its branch highlighted and its cause line added; 4.7 shows the Moffatt labeling; the Summary shows the complete tree with all five cause lines.
