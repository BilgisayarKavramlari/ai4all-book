## 4. Misconceptions (refutation pattern; six)

| # | Misconception | Where | Refutation, without creating a new one |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | "Hallucinations are rare glitches that the next update will remove" | 4.2 | They follow from producing likely text and from patterns joined without a truth check; scoring that rewards guessing adds to them (Kalai et al., 2025; Deep 4.A). Newer models, retrieval and tools reduce them on many tasks; as of 2026 none removes them |
| M2 | "A polite, neutral-sounding answer cannot be biased; bias is a problem of hiring and face-recognition systems" | 4.3 | Generated text inherits patterns from its training data and feedback; covert dialect bias in 2023–2024 models (Hofmann et al., 2024) |
| M3 | "Once I have given the tool a piece of information, it will use it for the rest of the conversation" | 4.4 | Long, piece-by-piece conversations lose constraints; early assumptions persist (Laban et al., 2025). Distinct from Ch. 3's single long input |
| M4 | "If the tool sounds sure, or agrees when I push back, it is probably right" | 4.5 | Tone and agreement can follow training pressures rather than evidence (OpenAI, 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). Not "confidence is meaningless": a weak signal that you still check (Ch. 8) |
| M5 | "A document or web page I give the tool is only data; it cannot change what the tool does" | 4.6 | Instructions and data arrive in the same token stream (Ch. 3), so text inside a document can act as an instruction (Greshake et al., 2023) |
| M6 | "When an AI tool makes the mistake, the tool, not the company or person using it, is responsible" | 4.7 | The tribunal rejected the airline's argument that it was not liable for its chatbot and held it responsible for all information on its website (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024, paras. 27–28). Scope: one small-claims decision in British Columbia (Ch. 18 for law) |

## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question, answered in the Summary:** *Is AI failure a bug that will be fixed, or a feature of how these systems work?* Answer (hedged): each branch of the tree follows from how the systems are built and used, so failures are expected, not exceptional; better models, retrieval and tools lower some rates on some tasks; whether an error becomes a harm depends on the people and organizations around the tool.

**Opening Case (450–600 words): *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149.** Facts only from the decision: on November 11, 2022, after their grandmother died, Jake Moffatt asked a support chatbot on Air Canada's website about bereavement fares (paras. 13–15). The reply said they could submit a ticket for a reduced bereavement rate within 90 days of the ticket's issue date, including after travel (para. 15, paraphrased). The words "bereavement fares" in that reply linked to the airline's own policy page, which said the policy does not apply after travel is completed (paras. 16–17). Moffatt booked flights on November 11 and 16 (para. 18), applied on November 17 (para. 20), and in February 2023 an airline representative admitted the chatbot had used "misleading words" (para. 22). Air Canada argued that it could not be held liable for the chatbot (paras. 4, 27). On February 14, 2024, Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers found negligent misrepresentation and ordered the airline to pay $812.02: $650.88 in damages, $36.14 in pre-judgment interest and $125 in tribunal fees (paras. 32, 40–44). Air Canada gave no information about the nature of its chatbot (para. 14); the decision does not say it was generative AI. The decision's pronouns for Moffatt (they/them); Canadian dollars.
**Chapter question:** "The chatbot was wrong and the airline's own page was right. Why would a chatbot say that, and who answers for it?" The tribunal answered the second half; 4.2 and 4.7 answer the first as far as the evidence allows.

**Prediction before the evidence (4.2):** "A model trained only on true sentences: can it still produce false ones?" Answered by the computed toy (F4.4).

**How the tree is built.** Signature 5 is drawn in full in 4.1 (branches in the visual guide's order: hallucination, bias, context loss, overconfidence, security vulnerability; plus a plain "other" box). Each of 4.2–4.6 shows the same tree with its branch highlighted and its cause line added. The Case Study shows Moffatt labeled on it; the Summary shows the complete tree with all five cause lines.

**Two tools, one uncertainty pair.** The **tree** labels output and system failures. The **failure-to-harm chain** (4.7) labels human and workflow failures (no check, overreliance, inappropriate delegation, a user leaking data) on one of its links. Every label and every cause link is **documented** (drawn solid) or **inferred** (drawn dashed). Rule: a label is inferred when the evidence does not show the branch's defining feature (Moffatt: the record does not show that the reply was generated, so "hallucination" is inferred). Overconfidence is labeled only with evidence about expressed certainty (a stated percentage, words such as "guaranteed", or a flip under pushback).
