| # | Misconception | Where confronted |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | "If an AI tool ran the numbers, the arithmetic and the method are right." | 11.4 (a tool's code or formula is a method someone must check; tool defaults change data silently, Ziemann et al., 2016); Case Study short 1. **Refutation check:** must not create "AI analysis is worse than a person's" — short 1 (Reinhart–Rogoff) is a human spreadsheet error found by outside replication, so the lesson is "check any analysis" |
| M2 | "A few blank cells don't change the answer, and if several ways of filling them agree, the answer is safe." | 11.2 demo: two blanks in 18 rows; five common ways of handling them all picked the same wrong event. **Refutation check:** must not create "never fill a blank" — the rule is: count blanks by group, ask why, get the real value first; only then compare defensible choices, and report that agreement is not correctness |
| M3 | "If race or gender is left out of the data, the model's scores cannot be biased." | Opening Case, 11.3 (Obermeyer: race excluded from the inputs; the label carried the gap). Named contrast with Ch. 2's proxy in the inputs |
| M4 | "A measure that predicts well is a good measure of what we care about." | 11.3 (cost was predicted well and still stood in badly for need; the authors report all three label choices performed reasonably on several outcomes, Table 2) |

## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question, answered in the Summary:** *Can an AI-assisted analysis make a decision for you?* Answer (hedged): it can do the arithmetic, draft a matrix and propose an option in seconds, but every analysis rests on choices — what a blank means, what a number stands in for, how criteria are weighted — and each can change the answer. People reviewing a score also tend to follow it. So get the real values where you can, recalculate what the decision depends on, test whether another defensible choice changes it, and decide as the owner, with your reasons written down.

| 11.1 | Decision support, not decision replacement (≤ 650) | Core | In **decision support** an AI output — a summary, an analysis, a score, a recommendation — goes to a named person, the **decision owner**, who decides and answers for the result; when an output **triggers an action** with no person in between, it is an **automated decision**. | 11.2 | A blank is a decision (≤ 850) | Core | A **missing value** is not nothing: every way of handling it is an assumption about why it is missing. Treating blanks as zero (what a total divided by a total silently does), deleting incomplete rows (**listwise deletion**), and **imputation** (filling a blank with an estimate: the mean, the group's mean, the previous row) each rest on a different assumption. When the reason a value is missing is related to the value itself, every shortcut can mislead — and they can all agree. **Rule, in this order:** (1) count blanks by group; (2) ask why each is blank; (3) get the real value if you can (the sign-in sheet); (4) only then compare defensible choices, and say whether the decision changes. Agreement among methods is not evidence that they are right. **Computed demo (constructed data, data/ch11_missing_choices.csv):** true $ per volunteer hour — bake sale 18.0, book swap 19.6, car wash 22.5; the two blanks are the book swap's two longest, slowest shifts (14 h and 13 h). Blank = 0 → book swap 39.2; listwise deletion 24.2; overall mean 23.7; event mean 26.1; previous shift 26.5. **All five picked the book swap; the best event was the car wash.** **Simulation (1,000 constructed logs per condition, seed 11, data/ch11_missing_sim.csv):** when blanks fell at random in every event, blank = 0 picked the best event in about 51% of logs, deletion in about 92%, the three fills in 75–81%; when the blanks fell only at one event, mostly on its long shifts, every choice did worse (blank = 0 about 34%, deletion 87%, fills 61–73%). | 11.3 | What you measure versus what you mean (≤ 900) | Core | Every decision uses a measure that stands in for its goal. **Proxy** (Ch. 2 glossary wording). Two places a proxy can hide, named: a **proxy in the inputs** (Ch. 2: a clue such as a word standing in for gender) and a **proxy label** (new: a recorded outcome used as the label because it is easy to measure, standing in for the outcome you care about). A proxy fails where the gap between it and the goal **differs across people**; good prediction of the proxy cannot reveal that gap. Obermeyer worked in full: label = next year's cost; goal = health need; wedge = unequal access to care ($1,801 less spending at the same health); race excluded from the inputs, yet the scores were biased, because the proxy was the label; changing only the label nearly doubled the share of Black patients in the top 3% (14.1% → 26.7%). Measurement framing in one sentence: many decisions target things that cannot be observed directly (need, ability, risk), so someone chooses a measurable stand-in, and that choice is an assumption (Jacobs & Wallach, 2021). **Three questions for any proxy:** What do we mean?
