## 5. Concept sequence

**Session question, answered in the Summary:** *Can an AI-assisted analysis make a decision for you?* Answer (hedged): it can do the arithmetic, draft a matrix and propose an option in seconds, but every analysis rests on choices — what a blank means, what a number stands in for, how criteria are weighted — and each can change the answer. People reviewing a score also tend to follow it. So recalculate what the decision depends on, test whether another defensible choice flips it, and decide as the owner, with your reasons written down.

| 11.3 | What you measure versus what you mean (≤ 900) | Core | Every decision uses a measure that stands in for its goal. **Proxy** (Ch. 2 glossary wording). | 11.6 | Human judgment and automation bias (≤ 750) | Core | A human reviewer helps only if the reviewer can, and does, disagree. **Automation bias**: the tendency to over-rely on automated output — following it when it is wrong (a **commission error**) or missing what it did not flag (an **omission error**) (Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt, 2012; Skitka, Mosier & Burdick, 1999); it is Chapter 7's overreliance in a decision setting. Evidence, dated and exact: in a simulated flight-monitoring task, participants with a highly but not perfectly reliable aid made both kinds of error (Skitka et al., 1999); a review of 74 studies found erroneous advice more likely to be followed with clinical decision support than without (Goddard et al., 2012); automation bias appears in novices and experts and is not prevented by training or instructions alone (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010); 27 radiologists rated far fewer mammograms correctly when a purported AI suggested a wrong category (Dratsch et al., 2023).
