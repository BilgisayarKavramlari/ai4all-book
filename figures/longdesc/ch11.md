**F11.1 — Chapter map.** Title "Chapter 11 at a glance". Three columns of three boxes. Hidden assumptions (rectangles): 11.1 Decision support; 11.2 A blank is a decision; 11.3 Measure vs. meaning. Checking and deciding (rectangles): 11.4 Check an AI analysis; 11.5 Decision matrices; 11.6 Human judgment. Below, the AI Work Loop strip: Frame, Prompt, Verify (dashed rounded box), Decide (dark rounded box, thick border), Disclose. Caption line: the chapter strengthens Decide, then Verify. Last line: opening case, a health algorithm built without race as an input (Obermeyer et al., 2019).

**F11.2 — Storyboard.** A horizontal timeline with six events alternating above and below the line. 2013–2015: scores and health records from one academic hospital (article). Analysis (rounded): researchers compare scores with health; no contact with the maker (article). Before publication: the maker replicates the finding on 3.7 million patients (authors' report). Before publication: trying a health-and-cost label, 84% less bias by one measure (authors' report). Oct 24–25, 2019 (green): article online October 24, in the October 25 issue of Science (confirmed). Oct 25, 2019 (rounded): New York regulators write to UnitedHealth Group about Optum's Impact Pro (confirmed: their letter). Footer: tiers explained; the product's name and maker rest on the regulators' letter and news reports; what changed for patients is not on record.

**F11.3 — Decision roles.** A hexagon at top, "An AI output: a score, an analysis, a draft", with arrows to three rectangles. Informs: a person reads it among other evidence → rounded box "The decision owner: decides; answers for it"; note: e.g., a summary of the patient's record. Recommends (thick border): a person decides whether to follow it → "The decision owner"; note: above the 55th percentile, the doctor is prompted to consider enrollment. Triggers an action (risk fill, hatched in print, thick border): no person in between, an automated decision → dashed box "The action happens: answered for afterward"; note: above the 97th percentile, flagged for enrollment by the score alone.

**F11.4 — What you can inspect (section 11.4 in v2).** A rounded box "Your question and data" with arrows to three hexagons. Chat answer (produces text about the data or the decision) → dashed gray box "nothing but the answer"; note: recalculate everything you rely on. Footer: Chapter 12 names the kinds of tools; current features in Compass.

**F11.5 — Five choices (data chart; slides and Compass only in v2).** Grouped bars for six conditions, book swap (cross-hatched in print) beside car wash (diagonal hatch), values to two decimals. **F11.21 — Summary card.** Six rounded cards: Distinguish (informs, recommends, triggers; name the owner); Test a blank (count, ask why, get the real value, then compare); Evaluate a proxy (mean? measure? for whom is the gap bigger?); Verify an analysis (six places; recalculate outside the tool); Stress-test a matrix (margins and shortlists, not only the top); Design a review step that makes disagreement possible.

**F11.22 — Missing-data mechanisms (Deep).** Three rows of three boxes. MCAR (missing completely at random): a page of the sign-in sheet got wet → deletion loses data but does not tilt the answer (green). MAR (missing at random, given what you know): evening shifts were logged less often, and you know which were evenings → a fill that uses the known pattern can work (green). MNAR (missing not at random; dashed thick box): the longest shifts went unlogged because they were long → every simple fix can mislead: get the real value (risk). After Rubin (1976); examples constructed.
