## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**Argument maps (F23.3, F23.4, F23.5, F23.6, F23.8, F23.11, F23.13, F23.18), shared grammar.** Every map has the same layout. At the top, a rectangle labeled CLAIM holds the claim and, in italics, who makes it. Below, the left column, headed "+ FOR (reasons and premises)", holds up to four rectangles, each tagged "+ FOR n", with its text and source. A vertical line on the left margin joins them and ends in an arrowhead on the claim's left side: they support it. The right column, headed "− AGAINST (objections)", holds up to four rectangles tagged "− AGAINST n". An objection either joins a line on the right margin that ends in a flat bar on the claim's right side (it opposes the claim), or runs straight left to a reason on the same row, ending in a flat bar on that reason and labeled "denies" (it denies that premise). A dashed outline and dashed link mark an unstated premise or the book's reconstruction (inferred). A box with dark fill and "▶ this section" is the section's focus. The legend under each map repeats these rules; the footer reads "A map shows the structure of an argument, not who is right" unless noted.

- **F23.3 (template):** claim "The claim being argued". FOR 1: a reason that supports the claim. FOR 2 (dashed, inferred): a premise the argument needs but does not state. AGAINST 1 opposes the claim as a whole. AGAINST 2 denies FOR 2.
- **F23.4 (Elif's map):** claim "AI tutors can't understand students, so they can't help them" (a classmate). FOR 1: AI tools produce text by prediction, not by understanding (contested: 23.2). FOR 2 (dashed): only something that understands a student can help that student. AGAINST 1 (Elif) opposes the claim: help can be measured in a study. AGAINST 2 (Elif) denies FOR 2: practice questions and feedback help whether or not the tool understands.
- **F23.5 (Turing):** claim "Replace 'Can machines think?' with the imitation game" (Turing, 1950). FOR 1: "think" is too vague to settle by defining words. FOR 2: we grant other people minds on the evidence of behavior (Turing's reply). AGAINST: Lady Lovelace's objection (a machine only does what we tell it), opposing the claim; the objection from consciousness (a machine must feel, not only produce words), denying FOR 2; Searle (1980): a program could pass and still understand nothing, opposing the claim.
- **F23.6 (Chinese Room):** claim "Running the right program is not enough for understanding" (Searle, 1980). FOR 1: a program manipulates symbols by their form (syntax). FOR 2: form alone is not enough for meaning (semantics). FOR 3: the person in the room follows the rules and understands no Chinese. AGAINST 1, the brain simulator reply (simulate a Chinese speaker's neurons), opposes the claim. AGAINST 2, the robot reply, opposes the claim: it accepts FOR 2 and adds the world (a program linked to cameras and motors might understand); Searle answered that the camera signals would reach him as more symbols. AGAINST 3, the systems reply (the whole system understands), denies FOR 3 and is highlighted.
- **F23.8 (stochastic parrots):** claim "Language models produce linguistic form without meaning" (Bender & Koller, 2020; Bender et al., 2021). FOR 1: meaning links form to something outside language. FOR 2: a model trained only on text receives only form. AGAINST 1, conceptual role (Piantadosi & Hill, 2022), denies FOR 1. AGAINST 2 (dashed, inferred): we grant understanding on the evidence of behavior, as with other people (Turing's 1950 reply, applied here by this book), opposing the claim. Footer: between the sides, Mitchell & Krakauer (2023) survey both, and Mahowald et al. (2024) separate formal and functional competence.
- **F23.11 (moral status):** claim "AI welfare deserves serious attention now" (Long et al., 2024). FOR 1: a realistic chance some systems will soon be conscious or robustly agentic (Long et al., 2024). FOR 2: under uncertainty, take proportionate precautions (Birch, 2024). AGAINST 1: consciousness likely depends on being a living organism (Seth, 2025), denying FOR 1. AGAINST 2: welfare talk is premature and feeds belief in seemingly conscious AI (Suleyman, 2025; interested source), opposing the claim. Footer: contested; another interested source, Anthropic, cited welfare uncertainty in August 2025; this book was drafted with Anthropic's tools.
- **F23.13 (responsibility gap):** claim "Learning systems open a responsibility gap: stop using them or accept the gap" (Matthias, 2004). FOR 1: holding someone responsible needs control and foresight. FOR 2: learning systems act in ways makers and operators cannot predict. AGAINST 1: responsibility practices are flexible and can stretch to cover new cases (Tigard, 2021), denying FOR 1. AGAINST 2: a third option, design for meaningful human control (tracking and tracing) (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018), opposing the claim's either/or, not denying that a gap can open. Footer: Elish (2019) is not drawn, because her moral crumple zone is a warning about where blame lands, not a denial of the gap.
- **F23.18 (long-term risk):** claim "Extinction risk from AI should be a global priority" (CAIS statement, 2023, paraphrased). FOR 1: capabilities are improving fast, and systems may pursue goals in ways people cannot control (Bengio et al., 2024). FOR 2: many AI researchers give non-trivial chances to extremely bad outcomes (Grace et al., survey 2023; forecast). AGAINST 1: fast capability gains do not mean fast effects, because effects arrive through adoption (Narayanan & Kapoor, 2025), denying FOR 1. AGAINST 2: the framing draws attention from present harms (Gebru & Torres, 2024), opposing the claim. Footer: the disputed premises are how fast gains become effects and whether such systems can be kept under control.

**F23.2 (storyboard).** A horizontal timeline with seven boxes alternating above and below: 1950 Turing proposes the imitation game; 1966 ELIZA, a pattern-matching chat program; 1980 Searle's Chinese Room; 2020 Bender and Koller: form is not meaning; 2021 the "stochastic parrots" paper; 2023 the consciousness indicators report; 2026 its authors say two models pass a three-party test. All confirmed (published texts); sources listed under the line.

**F23.7 (chart).** Six bars, percent of five-minute games in which the system was judged to be the human (Jones & Bergen, 2026): GPT-4.5 with a persona prompt 73%; LLaMa-3.1 with the persona prompt 56%; the same two without it, GPT-4.5 36% and LLaMa-3.1 38% (order confirmed from the PNAS full text and Table 1); baselines ELIZA 23% and GPT-4o 21%. A horizontal line at 50% marks being chosen as often as the real human. Persona bars are dark; no-persona bars hatched; baselines dotted.

**F23.9 (method).** Three boxes in a row with arrows: scientific theories of consciousness (for example global workspace, recurrent processing, higher-order) → indicator properties stated in computational terms → check a system's design. An arrow down leads to "a credence, not a verdict". A separate box: the system's own words ("I feel…") are a claim to check, which both sides accept. Notes: the method of Butlin et al. (2023; 2026); their 2023 conclusion; contested (Seth, 2025).

**F23.10 (chart).** One horizontal bar split into 33% "definitely not" an experiencer and 67% "some possibility" of phenomenal consciousness; 300 U.S. adults, July 2023 (Colombatto & Fleming, 2024). Source line: a measure of public belief, not evidence about ChatGPT.

**F23.12 (four gaps).** Title: four responsibility gaps ask four different questions. Four boxes in a two-by-two grid, each with a question and a fix: culpability (can anyone fairly be blamed? fix: clear duties before use); moral accountability (can anyone explain and answer for what the system did? fix: reasons people can give and check); public accountability (can officials or companies be called to account? fix: records, audits, a route to complain); active responsibility (does anyone see preventing harm as their job? fix: named owners, forward-looking duties). After Santoni de Sio & Mecacci (2021); the fix lines summarize their proposals; the gaps have different sources and can arise with non-learning systems too.

**F23.14 (chain).** Chapter 4's failure-to-harm chain for the 2018 Tempe crash, every link documented by the NTSB: 1, the output was wrong (detected 5.6 seconds before impact, never classified as a pedestrian); 2, checked before use? no (the design relied on the operator to brake; she was looking at a phone); 3, someone relies on it (automation complacency not addressed by the company); 4, loss (a pedestrian was killed). Links 2 and 3 have thick borders. Tag: all links documented (NTSB report HAR-19-03); tree: other (misclassification). The caption asks who answered for it, without settling it.

**F23.15 (three questions).** Three panels: Creative? (new, surprising and valuable: a property of the work; Boden, 2004); Whose choices? (who made the choices that shaped it: authorship in the philosophical sense); Who answers? (who can explain, defend and stand behind it: ownership, Chapter 10). A bar beneath: legal authorship is a fourth, separate question (Chapter 18).

**F23.16 (chart).** Grouped bars, percent increase versus writers with no AI ideas (Doshi & Hauser, 2024): novelty +5.4% (one AI idea) and +8.1% (five ideas); usefulness +3.7% and +9.0%; similarity to other stories, as a share of the no-AI range, +10.7% and +8.9%. One-idea bars hatched in gray, five-idea bars dark.

**F23.17 (table).** Columns: study, question asked, result as of its date, evidence status. Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025): did an assistant change support agents' output? +15% issues per hour, about +30% for less experienced, lower-skilled agents; measured (one firm). Humlum & Vestergaard (revised March 2026): did chatbots change earnings and hours in Denmark? effects larger than 2% ruled out two years after launch; tasks reorganized; measured (national records). Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (revised August 12, 2026): how has young workers' employment moved in AI-exposed jobs? ages 22–25 19% below less-exposed peers' pace to June 2026; measured, descriptive, not causal. Note: the numbers cannot be compared directly.

**F23.19 (document kinds).** Three columns. Statement (CAIS, May 2023): signatories including researchers and company leaders; one sentence; asks that extinction risk be a global priority. Assessment (International AI Safety Report, February 2026): developed with 100+ experts, backed by 30+ countries and international bodies; a review of evidence with disagreements recorded; endorses no policy. Essay (Narayanan & Kapoor, April 2025): two computer scientists; an argument from how past technologies spread; asks for resilience policies. Notes: each makes or reviews forecasts; a signature list shows who holds a view, not that it is true.

**F23.20 (position paper card).** Eight boxes: 1 the question; 2 the map; 3 side A at its strongest, from a primary source; 4 side B likewise; 5 hardest objection and your reply; 6 what would change it; 7 AI Use Declaration; and a dark box, YOUR VIEW (labeled), "undecided, because…" counts. Note: parts 3 and 4 never contain your view.

**F23.21 (funnel reuse).** Chapter 8's Verification Funnel with the claims and primary-source stages highlighted: the claim is a tool's sentence "Searle argued that …"; the independent check is a source-pack page or the library copy; the primary source is Searle (1980) itself. Note: an attributed claim is checked in the text it is attributed to.

**F23.22 (summary card).** Six cards, one per debate, each naming where it turns: understanding (is behavior enough evidence?); consciousness (can computation be enough, and what counts as evidence?); responsibility (can anyone be justly held responsible when no one could foresee?); creativity (new, surprising and valuable, or someone's choices?); work (which tasks change, and who decides?); long-term risk (how fast do effects arrive, can such systems be controlled, and what comes first?).
