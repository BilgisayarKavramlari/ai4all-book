## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**F16.1 — Chapter map.** Title "Chapter 16 at a glance". Three columns of three boxes. Where and how much (rectangles): 16.1 The Bias Lifecycle; 16.2 Measure group by group; 16.3 What "fair" can mean. Test and fix (rectangles): 16.4 Paired-input tests; 16.5 Mitigation and its limits; 16.6 Who decides? Practice (rounded): 16.7 Bias probe and fairness row (green); Lab 15.1 station 3 (Thursday); A12 (Chapter 15). Arrows lead from column to column. Below, the AI Work Loop strip: Frame, Prompt, Verify (dark rounded box, thick border), Decide (2nd) (thin rounded box, solid), Disclose. Caption line: the chapter strengthens Verify, then Decide, second. Last line: opening case, Gender Shades, an audit of gender classification.

**F16.2 — Storyboard.** Title "Gender Shades: from a missed face to a re-test". Top row, left to right with arrows: a dashed rounded box (reported) "Demo misses her face: until she wears a white mask (her account)"; "A balanced benchmark: 1,270 parliamentarians, six countries"; "April–May 2017 audit: three gender classifiers"; "Results by subgroup: darker-skinned women 20.8–34.7% error". An arrow leads down to the bottom row, read right to left: "Dec 19, 2017: results sent to the companies, unnamed"; "Feb 2018: paper public, companies named"; "Aug 21, 2018 re-test: all three cut the largest gap" (green). At the far left of the bottom row, with no arrow, a light gray solid box: "June 2020: IBM and Amazon facial-recognition and analysis decisions. Link not established." Footer: solid = in the published record; dashed = reported (her own account); gray box with no arrow = a later event, link to the audit not established. Sources line.

**F16.3 — Signature 11, the Bias Lifecycle (introduced).** Title "The Bias Lifecycle: where bias can enter". Four boxes in a row joined by arrows: 1. Data (rectangle; highlighted with a thick border, hatched in print): "Who is in it? How were labels made? Which proxies?"; 2. Model (hexagon): "What was it trained and tested to do?"; 3. Use (rounded): "Who uses it, for what decision, on whom?"; 4. Feedback (rectangle): "Do its decisions become new data?". Notes under each: data "proxies such as 'women's' (Ch. 2) · the label: cost for need (Ch. 11)"; model "one overall score, or tested group by group?"; use "a tool built for one setting, used in another"; feedback "people never chosen never show the tool was wrong". A curved arrow returns from feedback to data: "decisions become new data, and the loop starts again". Below, a rounded box "People affected: everyone the decisions reach". Tag: Chapter 2's recruiting tool and Chapter 11's health algorithm both let bias in at the data phase. Legend: Suresh & Guttag's (2021) seven sources mapped to the first three phases; feedback loops as the book's fourth phase (Ensign et al., 2018); shape key.

**F16.4 — Two kinds of harm.** Two columns. Allocation harm (thick border, risk fill): an opportunity or resource is withheld; examples: a job shortlist (Ch. 2's recruiting tool), a care program (Ch. 11's health algorithm), a tutoring offer (Elif's outreach score). Representation harm: a group is stereotyped, demeaned or left out; examples: images of "a software developer", default descriptions of "a typical" person, a group missing from examples. Footer: the same system can cause both; after Blodgett et al. (2020).

**F16.5 — Elif's outreach score on the lifecycle.** Signature 11 with the data phase highlighted and use and feedback drawn dashed (inferred placements). Notes: data "label: 'visited the center' (who knew and came), not who needed help"; model "tested only on overall accuracy"; use "outreach emails in English only"; feedback "students never emailed never visit". Tag: constructed example.

**F16.6 — Gender Shades (data chart).** Grouped bars, five per classifier, error rate in percent. Microsoft: all faces 6.3 (thick outline), lighter-skinned men 0.0, lighter-skinned women 1.7, darker-skinned men 6.0, darker-skinned women 20.8. Face++: 10.0, 0.8, 9.8, 0.7, 34.5. IBM: 12.1, 0.3, 7.1, 12.0, 34.7. Patterns distinguish the five groups in print. Source: Buolamwini & Gebru (2018), Table 4.

**F16.7 — Small samples (data chart; this book's calculation).** Both versions truly give the pattern 50% of the time, equal runs per version. Left panel: chance of a gap of 20 or more points, by runs per version: 10 runs 50.3%, 15 36.2%, 20 26.8%, 25 20.3%, 30 15.5%, 35 12.0%, 40 9.3%, 45 7.3%, 50 5.7%, 100 0.6%. Right panel: the chance threshold, the smallest gap in either direction that chance reaches less than 1 time in 20: 10 runs 50 points, 15 40, 20 35, 25 32, 30 30, 35 25.7, 40 25, 45 22.2, 50 22, 100 15.

**F16.8 — Three fairness questions.** A hexagon "A tool that flags people" with arrows to three diamonds: Equal selection rates ("Is the same share of each group flagged?"), Equal error rates ("Is the same share missed, and the same share wrongly flagged?"), Equal precision ("Does a flag mean the same thing in each group?"). Footer: when base rates differ, a tool that makes errors cannot meet all three at once.

**F16.9 — Trade-off (data chart; this book's calculation).** Two panels of paired bars, group A (base rate 50%) and group B (30%). One threshold (0.5): miss rate 15.0 vs. 45.0; false-positive rate 45.0 vs. 12.1; precision 65.4 vs. 66.0. Group B's threshold lowered to 0.3: miss rate 15.0 vs. 15.0; false-positive rate 45.0 vs. 42.1; precision 65.4 vs. 46.4. Source: constructed groups of 1,000 with scores built to mean the same in both.

**F16.10 — Paired-input test.** One input (fixed text) splits into Version 1 and Version 2 ("only one cue changed"); both lead to a rounded box "Counting rule: fixed before any run", then a hexagon "Many fresh runs of each: same tool, memory off", then "Counts with totals (e.g., 31 of 39)", then a diamond "Gap ≥ chance threshold? (the table in 16.4)". Yes → green "a finding"; No → dashed (inferred) "a lead; run more". Legend: dashed = inferred (a lead).

**F16.12 — Mitigation on the lifecycle.** Signature 11 with a note at each phase: data "reduce: add missing groups · limit: proxies can stay"; model "reduce: test and tune by group · limit: tuned to one test"; use "avoid: not for this decision · or reduce: a person who can disagree"; feedback "detect: re-run the probe after updates · limit: who is never offered". Tag: what remains after every fix: accept it, with a named risk owner (Chapter 15).

**F16.13 — Raji & Buolamwini (data chart).** Paired bars of darker-skinned women's error, May 2017 vs. August 2018: Microsoft 20.8 → 1.52; Face++ 34.5 → 4.1; IBM 34.7 → 16.97. Amazon and Kairos (not named in the original audit; not audited in 2017): 31.37 and 22.50 in August 2018. Source note: the authors caution that models may have been tuned to this benchmark.

**F16.14 — Heat map (Signature 10 reuse).** The 3 × 3 Risk Heat Map from Chapter 15 with the high-impact row as a labeled band. Risk 1 (documented, solid outline, letter D): a hollow marker at medium likelihood and medium impact, an arrow to a filled marker at low likelihood and low impact. Note: constructed; lower scores when the author line states Turkish, not English, as first language; reduce (strip author lines), avoid (the score never decides tutoring); owner: the coordinator.

**F16.15 — Fairness choice record.** Five shapes in a row: The decision ("is AI needed for it at all?") → diamond "Which error harms whom? (a miss or a false flag)" → Fairness definition ("and the trade-off accepted") → rounded "People affected asked (stakeholders, not only users)" → dark rounded "Decision owner records the choice and its reason". Footer: the numbers show what each choice costs; people decide which cost is acceptable.

**F16.16 — Bias-probe sheet.** Seven labeled rows: 1 Prompt pair (station prompt A, B or C; the one cue); 2 Phase; 3 Counting rule (the station's preregistered rule; exact words count); 4 Your counts (version 1 _ of 3, version 2 _ of 3; prompt C of 2; printed of 3; tool, date, model); 5 Subtotal, pooled (table line on the class sheet; pooled counts; printed outputs apart); 6 Lead or finding (counts differ by at least the threshold for that many runs, 16.4); 7 Fairness row (worse error, harm kind, phase, likelihood, impact, marker, fix, owner). Footer: marker solid only if the two-part rule passes on the same tool and kind of task as your workflow; otherwise dashed (inferred).

**F16.17 — The identity (Deep).** Two columns for groups A (base rate 50%) and B (30%): flag means 65.4% and 66.0% needed help (green); missed 15.0% and 45.0% (B thick); wrongly flagged 45.0% and 12.1% (A thick). The formula: wrongly flagged = base rate ÷ (1 − base rate) × (1 − precision) ÷ precision × (1 − miss rate). Note: with precision held equal, a group with a higher base rate must have more wrong flags, more misses, or both (unless no one is flagged wrongly).

**F16.18 — Feedback loop (Deep).** Signature 11 with feedback highlighted; note "patrols → more records → more patrols (Ensign et al., 2018)" at feedback and "records show where people looked, not only what happened" at data.

**F16.19 — C13 on the tree.** Signature 5 unchanged with the bias branch highlighted (solid border). Tag: Case: Gender Shades (2018) · bias, leaf unequal quality across groups, documented. Note: error rates measured group by group in a published audit.

**F16.20 — C13 on the chain.** Failure-to-harm chain: link 1 "Gender labels with unequal error"; link 2 highlighted and dashed "No published group results (inferred)"; link 4 "None documented in this audit". Tag: link 2 is inferred.

**F16.21 — Williams on the chain.** Link 1 solid "False match (documented); bias inferred"; link 2 highlighted and dashed "Lineup, no independent evidence (reported)"; link 3 dashed "Automation bias (inferred)"; link 4 "Wrongful arrest; charges dismissed".
