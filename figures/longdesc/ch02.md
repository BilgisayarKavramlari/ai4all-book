## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**F2.4 — Data-to-Prediction Pipeline (Signature 3).** Four boxes from left to right, joined by arrows. 1 Collect data, a rectangle (step): "Which examples? From whom?" 2 Label, a rounded box (human decision): "Who decides the answers? By which guideline?" 3 Train, a hexagon (AI component): "Which model? How long?" 4 Predict, a hexagon: "On which new cases?" Below them, a dashed rectangle, 5 Feedback: "Decisions based on predictions become new data", with a curved arrow from Predict into Feedback and another from Feedback back to Collect data. In the variants, the problem stage has a thick border (color: dark orange fill; print: light hatching) and a bold note beneath it: 2.4b "Who is in the sample?" under Collect data; 2.4c "Human decisions or recorded outcomes, often from the past" under Label; 2.4d three stages marked, "Mostly resumes from men" (Collect), "Possibly past hiring decisions" (Label), "Proxy words such as 'women's'" (Train).

**F2.5 — Population and sample.** A large rounded panel titled "Population: everything the model will meet in the real world" is scattered with outlined circles, triangles and squares. Inside it, a dashed box titled "Sample: the examples we have" holds 18 filled shapes: 16 circles and 2 squares, no triangles, captioned "mostly one kind". Below, three labels: Coverage gap, never sampled (no triangles in the sample); Non-response, asked but did not answer; Selection, entered only by meeting a condition. Key: filled shapes are in the sample; outlines are in the world but not in the sample.

**F2.9 — Label regimes (computed).** Grouped bars for four training conditions; the first bar of each pair is accuracy on all test examples, the second (lighter, hatched in print) is accuracy on the 8s, the digit made rare in the imbalanced condition and mislabeled in the one-direction condition. Balanced, correct labels: 98.0% and 98.5%. Imbalanced (10 vs. 60 examples): 91.7% and 77.3%. 20% wrong labels, scattered: 91.9% and 89.9%. 20% of all labels wrong in one direction (36 of the 60 eights labeled "3"): 74.7% and 24.3%. Thin lines show ±1 standard deviation across 30 runs. Source: the book's experiment on handwritten 3s, 5s and 8s (Alpaydin & Kaynak, 1998), logistic regression.

**F2.10 — Overfitting (computed).** Horizontal axis: model complexity, decision-tree depth 1 to 20. Two lines. Training accuracy (solid, round markers) climbs from about 20% at depth 1 to 93% at depth 8 and 100% from depth 14 on. Test accuracy (dashed, square markers) climbs from about 20% to 83% at depth 8 and then stays between 84% and 85%. From depth 11 the background is shaded and labeled "Extra complexity goes into memorizing". Source: the book's experiment on all ten handwritten digits, averages of 10 runs.

**F2.13 — Timeline.** Seven events evenly spaced (not to scale): 1956 Dartmouth workshop (term used in its 1955 proposal); 1980s expert systems; 1997 Deep Blue beats the world chess champion; 2012 a deep network leaps ahead in image recognition; 2016 AlphaGo beats a top Go player; 2017 the Transformer; 2022 ChatGPT released to the public. A band below groups them into Wave 1 (rules, logic, early learning), Wave 2 (deep learning) and Wave 3 (generative). Three boxes: Data (huge amounts of digital text and images), Compute (cheap parallel processing on graphics chips and computer clusters), Algorithms (training methods for very large networks).

**F2.14 — One learning step.** A U-shaped curve, titled "Each step moves the setting toward lower error", shows the squared error (× 1,000) for each value of the single setting w in the model price = w × size, for a 100-square-meter apartment that sold for 300 (thousand dollars). A filled dot at w = 2 is labeled "Start: w = 2, predicts 200, error 100". An arrow labeled "One step downhill" leads to a hollow dot near w = 2.6. A triangle at w = 3 marks the lowest error. Illustration, not market data.
