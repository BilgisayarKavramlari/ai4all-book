## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**F12.1 — Chapter map.** Title "Chapter 12 at a glance". Three columns of three boxes. What a tool is (rectangles): 12.1 Fit: a task and a tool together; 12.2 Kinds of tools; 12.3 What studies of tool effects show. Testing and deciding (rectangles): 12.4–12.5 Total cost and the data gate; 12.6 A fair bake-off; 12.7–12.8 Fit matrix and re-testing. Practice (rounded): 12.9 Lab 12.1 and A10 (Studio, green); Labs 12.2–12.3 (optional, dashed); Case Study: the BCG consultants. Arrows lead from the first column to the second and the second to the third. Below, the AI Work Loop as a strip: Frame (highlighted, thick border), Prompt, Verify (dashed), Decide, Disclose. Notes: the chapter strengthens Frame, then Verify; opening case: consultants who used one tool on many tasks (BCG, 2023), revisited.

**F12.2 — Design of the BCG experiment.** Left: a rectangle "Baseline task, no AI, all 758". An arrow leads to a diamond "Random assignment", which branches to three groups: a rounded box "No AI" and two hexagons "GPT-4" and "GPT-4 + prompting overview". From the groups, arrows lead to two rectangles: "Inside the frontier: 18 subtasks, 385 consultants" and "Outside the frontier: one task, 373 consultants" (thick border). A green rectangle at the bottom: "Measured: correctness, rated quality, time". Notes: everyone with AI used the same model (GPT-4, end of April 2023) with the provider's default options; source Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013; Organization Science (2026).

**F12.3 — One part, two tools.** The Jagged Frontier Map from Chapter 1, unchanged: a jagged line over tasks ordered by how hard they look to a person, with six numbered example tasks, three usually inside (filled circles) and three usually outside (open circles). Below the map, a heading "Sam's unit conversion, 41 oz-in to N·m: the same part, two tools (constructed example)" and two boxes. Left, dashed: "Tool A, no calculation: outside (open circle). Gave 2.9 N·m, ten times too large; the right answer is about 0.29 N·m." Right, thick: "Tool B, runs code: inside (filled circle). Matched his hand calculation on one known-answer test; one run, so test more."

**F12.5 — Anatomy of a tool.** A yellow hexagon "Model, settings (parameters)" in the center, connected by plain lines (no arrows) to four rectangles: above, "What it receives: your prompt, files you upload, your mail and documents, web pages it retrieves"; left, "What it can do: produce text, search, run code, act for you (Chapter 14)"; right, "Where it runs: provider's service, institution's license, hardware you or your institution control"; below, with a thick border, "Whose terms cover your input: used for training? kept how long, seen by whom? consumer or institutional contract". Note: two tools built on the same model can differ in all four; the thick border marks the part that decides the data gate.

**F12.7 — METR chart.** Horizontal bars, change in time per task with AI allowed, from −60% (faster) to +60% (slower). Two dashed light bars to the left: forecast before the study, 24% less time; belief after the study, 20% less time. One solid bar to the right (risk color, cross-hatched in print): measured, 19% more time (confidence interval +2% to +39%). Source: Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein (2025), 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks, February–June 2025; results for early-2025 tools in this setting.

**F12.8 — Total cost of use.** Above a horizontal line, headed "What you see": two rectangles, "Price" and "Usage limits". Below it, headed "What you also pay": four rectangles, "Checking time: verification cost, often the largest" (thick border, risk fill, hatched in print), "Learning time", "Data you give up (12.5)" and "Switching cost: lock-in (12.8)". On the right, a rounded blue box "No-AI baseline: the time the part takes you without a tool, done to your criteria", with an arrow labeled "compare" pointing at the cost boxes. Note: total cost of use is everything above and below the line.

**F12.9 — The data gate.** A rectangle "Data class of the input: UTD's Public, Controlled or Confidential Data; also personal data about others, and your own work" leads to a diamond "Do the tool's data terms allow this class?". Yes leads right to a green rectangle "Pass: now test quality (the bake-off, 12.6)". No leads down to a second diamond "Can you change the input?", with a note "for example: remove names, use placeholders, leave out confidential details". Yes leads up to the pass box; no leads down to a thick rectangle with risk fill (hatched in print): "Blocked for this part, whatever its quality". A side note lists the three questions about the terms: training; retention and access; consumer or institutional contract.

**F12.10 — Quality and correctness chart.** Two bar panels for the BCG task outside the frontier. Left, "Rated quality: up": GPT-4 only +17.9%, GPT-4 with the overview +25.1%, compared with the no-AI average. Right, "Correct answers: down (no AI: 84.4%)": GPT-4 only −13.9 percentage points, GPT-4 with the overview −24.5 points. Source: Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013, Table 6 and Table 4, column 1; quality rose for right and wrong answers alike (working paper, Figure 8).

**F12.11 — A fair bake-off.** Three rectangles in a row: "Same frame and criteria", "Same prompt, word for word", "Same test inputs: typical, different, edge"; beside them, "Only the tool changes". Arrows lead to three options: hexagons "Tool A: fresh chat, memory off; tool, model, date" and "Tool B: fresh chat, memory off; tool, model, date", and a rounded box "No AI: you, timed". All three lead to a green rectangle "Score separately: correctness (independent check), polish 1–5, time including checking", then to a thick rectangle "Verdict per input, not per tool". Note: the edge input matters most.

**F12.12 — Sam's fit matrix.** A grid of three parts (rows) by three options (columns: Tool A, institutional; Tool B, consumer; No AI). Sponsor thank-you emails: Tool A "Use with a check", automation, inside, chosen (thick, green); Tool B "Do not use: blocked", personal data (thick, risk fill, hatched in print); No AI "Do not use", floor, slower, predicted (dashed). Datasheet summaries: Tool A "Use, check every unit", automation, inside, chosen; Tool B "Only as a lead", automation, outside; No AI "Do not use", floor, 10 min each. Torque calculation: Tool A "Do not use", outside; Tool B "Use with a check", automation, inside, tested once; No AI "Use", floor, fastest, chosen. Legend: thick and filled = chosen; risk fill = blocked by the data gate; dashed = predicted, not tested; filled circle inside, open circle outside the tool's frontier (Signature 2); rung on the Delegation Ladder (Signature 7). Note: constructed example; re-test triggers.

**F12.13 — Sam's parts on the Delegation Ladder.** Signature 7 from Chapter 5, unchanged: an arrow labeled "Harder to see and check what the AI contributed before it takes effect"; rungs 3 Agency, 2 Augmentation, 1 Automation (highlighted), and the floor, No AI (highlighted), each with the AI's part in a hexagon. Notes under the rungs: automation, "Sam: sponsor emails; datasheet summaries (check every unit)"; floor, "Sam: torque calculation, in his own spreadsheet". Footer: Chapter 12, each part of a task gets its own rung, and sometimes the floor.

**F12.14 — Drift chart.** Grouped bars, correct answers on prime versus composite (%), for March 2023 (light, dotted in print) and June 2023 (dark, hatched in print): GPT-4 84% then 51%; GPT-3.5 50% then 76%. A dashed line at 50% is labeled "50% = guessing", because half the numbers were prime. Source: Chen, Zaharia and Zou (2024), 1,000 numbers, a "think step by step" prompt.

**F12.15 — Portability and re-test triggers.** Two columns. "Keep in your own files" (rounded, green): task frames and success criteria; prompts and test inputs; outputs you need, exported. "Stays with the tool unless you copy it" (dashed): chat history; standing instructions and memory; access tied to a school or job. Below, a thick rectangle "Re-test triggers: a model update or retirement, a change in price or terms, a new kind of input, a new job or school".

**F12.16 — A10 sheet.** Eight rows, each a labeled box with its contents: 1 Task (parts, predictions per tool, frame and criteria, data class); 2 Tools (kind, how the model is reached, model, date); 3 Data gate (three questions per tool, cited; pass, changed input or blocked; thick border); 4 Runs (route A, B or C; same prompt; typical, different, edge; fresh chats); 5 Scores (correctness with an independent check, polish 1–5, time including checking); 6 Cost row (price form, limits, checking time, no-AI baseline estimate); 7 Fit matrix (decision, rung and reason per cell; predicted or tested once; re-test trigger); 8 Judgment (80–100 words, one comparison with a 12.3 study; declaration with five elements).

**F12.17 — Three ways of working.** Three rounded boxes. Centaurs, about 14%: divide the work, deciding which parts go to the tool and keeping the rest; mapped (italic, this book's reading) to "a fit matrix in practice". Cyborgs, about 60%: work with the tool turn by turn, down to sentences and subtasks; mapped to augmentation. Self-automators, about 27% (thick border, risk fill): hand over the whole task in one or two exchanges, with little review; mapped to automation without its check. Sources: Dell'Acqua et al. (2023); shares from 244 consultants in Randazzo et al. (2025), as summarized by the authors.

**F12.18 — Tree.** Signature 5 from Chapter 4, unchanged, with the hallucination branch marked by a thick dashed border (inferred). Tag: "Case: BCG outside task (2023), hallucination (contradicts its input, or wrong reasoning), inferred (dashed)". Note: the task was designed so the tool would err; the paper does not report each consultant's outputs.

**F12.19 — Chain.** The failure-to-harm chain from Chapter 4: link 1 The output is wrong (dashed: inferred; note "answer went against the interview notes"); link 2 Checked before use? (thick; note "less questioning, authors' statement"); link 3 Someone relies on it (thick; note "wrong recommendation submitted"); link 4 Loss or harm (note "an experiment: no real-world harm"). Tag: links 2 and 3 documented by the authors' statement; Chapter 11 calls this pattern automation bias.

**F12.21 — Open weights and open source.** Three stacked layers: a hexagon "Parameters (weights): what you download and run"; "Code to train and run the system"; "Information about the training data: enough to build a similar system". A dashed box on the right: "License terms: what you may do, use, study, change, share; some licenses limit uses". A short bracket beside the top layer is labeled "open-weight release"; a long bracket beside all three is labeled "open source AI (OSI): all three". Note: Open Source AI Definition (OSI, 2024).
