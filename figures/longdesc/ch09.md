## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**F9.1 — Chapter map.** Title "Chapter 9 at a glance". Three columns of three boxes. Judging sources (rectangles): 9.1 Types and the ladder · 9.2 Status; 9.3 Read laterally; 9.4 Trace upstream. Reading with AI (rectangles): 9.5 Summary fidelity; 9.6 AI search and reports; 9.7 Cite what you read. Practice (rounded): 9.8 Lab 9.1 and A6 (Studio, green); Labs 9.2–9.3 (optional, dashed); Case Study: the Deloitte report. Arrows lead from the first column to the second and the second to the third. Below, the AI Work Loop as a strip: Frame, Prompt, Verify (highlighted, thick border), Decide, Disclose (dashed). Notes: the chapter strengthens Verify, then Disclose; opening case: a government report whose references did not hold up (Australia, 2025).

**F9.2 — Deloitte–DEWR storyboard.** A horizontal timeline with six events, alternating above and below the line. Dec 2024–Jun 2025: Deloitte conducts the review (confirmed). Aug 14, 2025: the department publishes the report, dated Jul 4 on its cover (confirmed). Aug 22, 2025 (rounded, a person's action): a newspaper reports errors an outside reader raised (reported). Oct 3, 2025 (green): corrected report released; AI use disclosed (confirmed). Oct 8, 2025: final installment of A$97,587.11 repaid (confirmed). Feb 3, 2026 (green): report updated again to address corrections (confirmed). Notes: confirmed = the department's and other official records, reported = news reports; sources DEWR (2025a, 2025b, 2026), Karp (2025), Parliament of Australia (2025); whether the AI tool chain produced the errors is not on record.

**F9.3 — Source types.** Three rectangles in a row. Primary (green): the record itself, a study, dataset, law, court record, official page. Secondary: reports on or interprets primary sources, a review, a textbook, a news story. Tertiary: collects and condenses, an encyclopedia, an index, a study guide. Under each, a rounded box saying what it is good evidence for: what was done, found or decided; what the records add up to, if it cites them; background and where to look next. Below, a wide rectangle: "Type depends on the claim: the Deloitte report", primary for what the department was told, secondary for what the court said (the judgment is the record). At right, a dashed hexagon: AI output, not a source of evidence, a lead. Note: gray literature can be any of the three types; ask who paid for it.

**F9.4 — Retellings (hop chain).** Six boxes left to right joined by arrows: Study (green, the record of what was done and found) → Preprint (posted before peer review) → Journal article (after peer review) → Press release (written for the media; dashed arrow into it) → News story (written by a journalist) → AI summary (hexagon, new text generated from a source; dashed arrow into it). Notes under the press release: exaggeration documented in many (Sumner et al., 2014); under the AI summary: broader claims documented (Peters & Chin-Yee, 2025). Legend: dashed box (when shown) = no record found; dashed arrow = drift documented at that step. Foot: not every claim travels every step; check status (9.2) and trace back (9.4).

**F9.6 — Vertical and lateral reading.** A rectangle at left, "An unfamiliar website", splits into two rows of three rounded boxes. Upper row, labeled Vertical reading: Stay on the page (read it carefully) → Judge it by its looks (logos, About page, .org, references) → Often taken in (thick border, risk; historians and students in the study). Lower row, labeled Lateral reading: Leave early (often within about half a minute) → Ask outside sources (who is behind it, and who pays?) → Return with evidence (green; fact-checkers: faster, more accurate). Source: after Wineburg & McGrew (2019), 10 fact-checkers, 10 historians, 25 undergraduates.

**F9.7 — The goldfish trace (hop chain).** Five boxes joined by arrows: AI answer (hexagon; "shorter than a goldfish's", no source given; note: below the ladder, a lead) → News articles (many repeat the claim, cite a 2015 report; note: exists, names its source) → Consumer report (Microsoft Canada, 2015 survey, cites a statistics website; note: exists, not the origin) → Statistics website (gives no study, did not answer requests; note: its listed sources found no research) → No study found (dashed box; dead end, verdict unsupported; note: write what the trail shows). Foot: Maya's trace, a constructed walk-through of a documented trail (Maybin, 2017).

**F9.8a — The funnel with the ladder's stages (Signature 8 reuse).** Chapter 8's Verification Funnel, unchanged: hexagon "An AI output" → Claims → 1. Independent check (highlighted) → 2. Primary source (highlighted) → 3. Expert (rounded), each stage with an arrow to a side rail "Stop when the evidence matches the stakes", which leads to Verdict (supported · partly supported · unsupported · contradicted · cannot verify), with a dashed box "Remaining uncertainty". Subtitle: Chapter 9 asks how directly a source at these stages records the claim.

**F9.8b — The source ladder.** Title "The source ladder: how directly does this source record the claim?". Two vertical rails with four rungs, top to bottom: 4. Is the record of this claim (green; the study for "this study found"; a systematic review for "what studies show"); 3. Cites the specific record (a findable citation, page, table or DOI, or a narrower record: one study for a general claim); 2. Names a source too vaguely to find ("a recent Harvard study", "experts say"); 1. Gives no source (an unsourced page, a social post, a quotation with no reference). Below the ladder, a dashed hexagon: AI output, a lead to sources, not evidence (except of what the tool produced). At right, brackets: rung 4 = funnel stage 2, the record of this claim; rungs 1–3 = funnel stage 1, an independent check if the author or publisher can be named (rungs 1–2 rarely enough). Notes: a citation lifts a source only if the cited record holds this claim (or a narrower part of it); opening the cited record does not raise this source's rung, it gives you a second source with its own rung. The reusable function can mark a rung (thick border) and draw an arrow at the left for "the rung your purpose needs".

**F9.9 — Summaries of medical abstracts (published values).** Horizontal bars, share of summaries containing generalized conclusions, by model, sorted: LLaMA 3.3 70B 89%; ChatGPT-4o 65%; LLaMA 2 70B 51%; GPT-4 Turbo 50%; DeepSeek 46%; ChatGPT-4.5 41%; Claude 3.7 Sonnet 40%; GPT-3.5 Turbo 34%; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 31%; Claude 2 19% (dark bar; the others are orange, cross-hatched in print). A dashed vertical line marks 20%, the share of the original abstracts with generalized conclusions. Title: in a 2025 test, most models produced generalized conclusions more often than the abstracts did. The source line notes that the authors found no significant difference from the originals for the Claude models. Source: Peters & Chin-Yee (2025), Table 2, 100 medical abstracts, simple prompt.

**F9.11 — Signature 4 for a summary.** Chapter 3's Anatomy of an LLM Response with the note "The summary is new text produced from the pasted original". In the context window, the "pasted or retrieved text" slot is highlighted; the sampling step is highlighted. The rest (tokens, the model scoring every possible next token, the loop back into the context) is unchanged.

**F9.12 — AI search citations (published values).** Three vertical bars, share of 200 queries answered incorrectly: Perplexity 37%, ChatGPT Search 67% (134 of 200), Grok-3 (beta) 94%. A dashed horizontal line at 60% is labeled "all eight tools together: more than 60%". Source: Jazwinska & Chandrasekar (2025), Tow Center, 200 news excerpts per tool, eight tools, tested in February 2025; one run each; the three tools whose rates the article text reports; error rates ranged from 37% to 94%.

**F9.13 — Research report.** Top row: rounded "Your question" → hexagon "Many searches" (many pages retrieved) → hexagon "A long report" (dozens of citations). From the report, lines lead down to four rounded checks: 1. Types (which rungs, for which claims?); 2. Support (does each page say it?); 3. Missing evidence (dates, counter-evidence, primary sources); 4. Faithfulness (does the synthesis match its sources?). An arrow leads to a dashed box "Remaining uncertainty: what you could not check, and why".

**F9.14 — What to cite.** Diamond "Did you open and read the source?": no → thick risk box "Do not cite it: read it first, or drop the claim"; yes → diamond "Are you quoting or paraphrasing AI output itself?": no → green box "Cite the source you read, not the tool that found it"; yes → green box "Cite the source, and cite the AI chat; APA 2025: company, date, chat title, [Generative AI chat], tool, link". A wide rounded box at the bottom: "In this book's assignments, declare AI use either way (AI Use Declaration, Chapter 1)".

**F9.15 — A6 review sheet.** Eight rounded boxes stacked, each with a description to its right: Question, purpose, claim (the claim you most need to support); Source: type and status (primary/secondary/tertiary; peer-reviewed, preprint, corrected, retracted or not reviewed); Outside-source log (who is behind it, according to sources it did not write or pay for); Rung for the claim · rung needed (green; with a one-sentence justification); Use decision (needs both: the rung and who is behind it, from outside sources); Trace (at least two hops or a dead end; three checks per hop; verdict); Summary check (each sentence: passage, verdict, drift type if one fits; omitted result); References · judgment · declaration (dashed; what you relied on, and what remains uncertain).

**F9.16 — GRADE, simplified (Deep).** Two rectangles at left, Randomized trials (start high) and Observational studies (start low), point to a rounded box "Certainty rating". From the rating, an arrow leads to a thick risk box "Move down: study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, reporting bias". A dashed arrow runs from Observational studies to a green box "Move up: mainly observational: a very large effect; dose-response; biases that would hide the effect". An arrow leads down to the four levels: High · Moderate · Low · Very low. Simplified from Guyatt et al. (2008) and Balshem et al. (2011).

**F9.20 — Deloitte on the failure-to-harm chain.** Chapter 4's chain: 1 hexagon "The output is wrong" (note: incorrect references reached the published report, confirmed) → 2 diamond "Checked before use?", thick solid border, the link that failed (documented; note: two checkpoints, firm and department; what ran is not on record) → "no" → 3 rounded "Someone relies on it" (note: published by the department; recommendations stand, reported) → 4 risk box "Loss or harm" (note: corrections, part of the fee repaid, public criticism, confirmed and reported). From the diamond, "yes" leads to a green box "Caught: fixed before harm". Bottom line: tiers, confirmed = the department's pages, reported = news reports, inferred = this book's reading.

**F9.18 — Summary card.** Six rounded cards in two rows: Differentiate (type and status of a source, for one claim); Place (the rung for the claim, and the rung you need); Read laterally (who is behind it decides how you use it); Trace (follow a claim upstream; name the drift); Judge (check a summary sentence by sentence); Critique AI research reports; cite what you read and declare AI use.
