## Long descriptions (complex figures)

**F9.1 — Chapter map.** Title "Chapter 9 at a glance". Three columns of three boxes. Judging sources (rectangles): 9.1–9.2 Types and status; 9.3 Read laterally; 9.4 Trace upstream. Reading with AI (rectangles): 9.5 Summary fidelity; 9.6 AI search and reports; 9.7 Cite what you read. Practice (rounded): 9.8 Lab 9.1 and A6 (Studio, green); Labs 9.2–9.3 (optional, dashed); Case Study: the Deloitte report. Arrows lead from the first column to the second and the second to the third. Below, the AI Work Loop as a strip: Frame, Prompt, Verify (highlighted, thick border), Decide, Disclose (dashed). Notes: the chapter strengthens Verify, then Disclose; opening case: a government report whose references did not hold up (Australia, 2025).

**F9.2 — Deloitte–DEWR storyboard.** A horizontal timeline with six events, alternating above and below the line. Dec 2024–Jun 2025: Deloitte conducts the review (confirmed). Aug 14, 2025: the department publishes the report (confirmed). From Aug 2025 (rounded, a person's action): an outside reader reports errors to the media (reported). Oct 3, 2025 (green): corrected report released (confirmed). Oct 2025: final instalment repaid, just over A$97,000 (reported). Feb 3, 2026 (green): report updated again to address corrections (confirmed). Notes: confirmed = the department's own pages, reported = news reports; sources DEWR (2025a, 2025b, 2026), Associated Press (2025), CFO Dive (2025); the corrected report disclosed AI use (reported); whether the AI tool produced the errors is not on record.

**F9.3 — Source types.** Three rectangles in a row. Primary (green): the record itself, a study, dataset, law, court record, official page. Secondary: reports on or interprets primary sources, a review, a textbook, a news story. Tertiary: collects and condenses, an encyclopedia, an index, a study guide. Under each, a rounded box saying what it is good evidence for: what was done, found or decided; what the records add up to, if it cites them; background and where to look next. Below, a wide rectangle: "Type depends on the claim: the Deloitte report", primary for what the department was told, secondary for what the court said (the judgment is the record). At right, a dashed hexagon: AI output, not a source of evidence, a lead. Note: gray literature can be any of the three types; ask who paid for it.

**F9.4 — Retellings (hop chain).** Six boxes left to right joined by arrows: Study (green, the record of what was done and found) → Preprint (posted before peer review) → Journal article (after peer review) → Press release (written for the media; dashed arrow into it) → News story (written by a journalist) → AI summary (hexagon, new text generated from a source; dashed arrow into it). Notes under the press release: exaggeration documented in many (Sumner et al., 2014); under the AI summary: broader claims documented (Peters & Chin-Yee, 2025). Legend: dashed arrow = drift documented at that step. Foot: not every claim travels every step; check status (9.2) and trace back (9.4).

**F9.6 — Vertical and lateral reading.** A rectangle at left, "An unfamiliar website", splits into two rows of three rounded boxes. Upper row, labeled Vertical reading: Stay on the page (read it carefully) → Judge it by its looks (logos, About page, .org, references) → Often taken in (thick border, risk; historians and students in the study). Lower row, labeled Lateral reading: Leave within seconds (open new tabs) → Ask outside sources (who is behind it, and who pays?) → Return with evidence (green; fact-checkers: faster, more accurate). Source: after Wineburg & McGrew (2019), 10 fact-checkers, 10 historians, 25 undergraduates.

**F9.7 — The goldfish trace (hop chain).** Five boxes joined by arrows: AI answer (hexagon; "shorter than a goldfish's", no source given; note: below the ladder, a lead) → News articles (many repeat the claim, cite a 2015 report; note: exists, names its source) → Marketing report (Microsoft Canada, 2015, credits a statistics website; note: exists, not the origin) → Statistics website (gives no study, did not answer requests; note: source material not provided) → No study found (dashed box; dead end, verdict unsupported; note: write what the trail shows). Foot: Elif's trace, a constructed walk-through of a documented trail (Maybin, 2017).

**F9.8a — The funnel with the ladder's stages (Signature 8 reuse).** Chapter 8's Verification Funnel, unchanged: hexagon "An AI output" → Claims → 1. Independent check (highlighted) → 2. Primary source (highlighted) → 3. Expert (rounded), each stage with an arrow to a side rail "Stop when the evidence matches the stakes", which leads to Verdict (supported · partly supported · unsupported · contradicted · cannot verify), with a dashed box "Remaining uncertainty". Subtitle: Chapter 9 asks how directly a source at these stages records the claim.

**F9.8b — The source ladder.** Title "The source ladder: how directly does this source record the claim?". Two vertical rails with four rungs, top to bottom: 4. Is the record itself (green; the study, dataset, law, court record or official page that holds the claim); 3. Cites the record specifically (you can open the record and check it: a review, a textbook, a referenced report); 2. Reports the claim and names a source (that you have not checked yet: a news story, a press release); 1. States the claim, no traceable origin (an unsourced page, a social post, a quotation with no reference). Below the ladder, a dashed hexagon: AI output, a lead to sources, not evidence (except of what the tool produced). At right, brackets: rung 4 = funnel stage 2, primary source; rungs 1–3 = funnel stage 1, independent check, if the author or publisher can be named. Notes: pick the rung your purpose and stakes need; the ladder measures how checkable a source is for one claim, not the strength of a body of evidence (Deep 9.A). The reusable function can mark a rung (thick border) and draw an arrow at the left for "the rung your purpose needs".

**F9.9 — Summaries of medical abstracts (published values).** Horizontal bars, share of summaries containing generalized conclusions, by model, sorted: LLaMA 3.3 70B 89%; ChatGPT-4o 65%; LLaMA 2 70B 51%; GPT-4 Turbo 50%; DeepSeek 46%; ChatGPT-4.5 41%; Claude 3.7 Sonnet 40%; GPT-3.5 Turbo 34%; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 31%; Claude 2 19% (dark bar; the others are orange, cross-hatched in print). A dashed vertical line marks 20%, the share of the original abstracts with generalized conclusions. Title: in a 2025 test, 9 of 10 models produced broader conclusions more often than the abstracts they summarized. Source: Peters & Chin-Yee (2025), Table 2, 100 medical abstracts, simple prompt.

**F9.11 — Signature 4 for a summary.** Chapter 3's Anatomy of an LLM Response with the note "The summary is new text produced from the pasted original". **F9.12 — AI search citations (published values).** Three vertical bars, share of 200 queries answered incorrectly: Perplexity 37%, ChatGPT Search 67% (134 of 200), Grok-3 (beta) 94%. A dashed horizontal line at 60% is labeled "all eight tools together: more than 60%". Source: Jaźwińska & Chandrasekar (2025), Tow Center, 200 news excerpts per tool, eight tools, tested in February 2025; three tools shown: the best, the worst and one with an exact count reported.

**F9.13 — Research report.** Top row: rounded "Your question" → hexagon "Many searches" (many pages retrieved) → hexagon "A long report" (dozens of citations). From the report, lines lead down to four rounded checks: 1. Types (which rungs, for which claims?); 2. Support (does each page say it?); 3. Missing evidence (dates, counter-evidence, primary sources); 4. Faithfulness (does the synthesis match its sources?). An arrow leads to a dashed box "Remaining uncertainty: what you could not check, and why".

**F9.14 — What to cite.** Diamond "Did you open and read the source?": no → thick risk box "Do not cite it: read it first, or drop the claim"; yes → diamond "Are you quoting or paraphrasing AI output itself?": no → green box "Cite the source you read, not the tool that found it"; yes → green box "Cite the source, and cite the AI chat; APA 2025: company, date, chat title, [Generative AI chat], tool, link". A wide rounded box at the bottom: "In this course, declare AI use either way (AI Use Declaration, Chapter 1)".

**F9.15 — A6 review sheet.** Eight rounded boxes stacked, each with a description to its right: Question, purpose, claim (the claim you most need to support); Source: type and status; Outside-source log (who is behind it, according to sources it did not write or pay for); Rung for the claim · rung needed (green; with a one-sentence justification); Use decision (rely · use with a caveat · only as a lead · do not use); Trace (at least two hops or a dead end; three checks per hop; verdict); Fidelity check (each summary sentence: passage, verdict, drift type); References · judgment · declaration (dashed; what you relied on, and what remains uncertain).

**F9.16 — GRADE, simplified (Deep).** Two rectangles at left, Randomized trials (start high) and Observational studies (start low), point to a rounded box "Certainty rating". From it, arrows to a thick risk box "Move down: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias" and a green box "Move up: for example, a very large effect", and down to a box listing the four levels: High · Moderate · Low · Very low. Simplified from Guyatt et al. (2008).
