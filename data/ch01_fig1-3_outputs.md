Practice problems by hand instead of just rereading notes. Chemistry is mostly applied skill, so work through stoichiometry, equilibrium, and gas law problems without looking at the solutions first. Then check your answers and redo the ones you missed.

Make a one-page sheet of key equations, units, and constants, and review it often. Also explain each reaction type or periodic trend in your own words. Where you can't, you've found a gap to study.

Do past exams or old quizzes under timed conditions, showing every step and unit. This builds speed and gets you used to exam pressure. Afterward, label each mistake as a concept error or a calculation slip, since they need different fixes.

## Panel B — Claude Sonnet 5.5 (claude-sonnet-5-5), 2026-10-17

1. Practice with problems, not just rereading. Work through past exams, textbook problems, and problem sets without looking at the solution first, then check where you went wrong. Chemistry is a skill, so stoichiometry, equilibrium, and mechanisms only stick when you do them yourself.

2. Use active recall and spaced repetition. Make flashcards for reactions, definitions, periodic trends, and formulas. Quiz yourself from memory, and review a little each day instead of cramming the night before.

3. Understand the concepts behind the formulas. Be able to explain why something happens, such as why bond polarity affects solubility. Study with a friend and teach topics to each other. If you can explain it simply, you know it. Also write out a one-page summary of key equations and concepts from memory.
