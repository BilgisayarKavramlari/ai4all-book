## Before the lab (about 15 minutes; the Your Turn in section 7.5)
Choose a concept that is new to you from a course you are taking this term, and a course source with **keyed questions** (questions that come with an answer key, such as textbook end-of-section or practice questions; in history or literature, study-guide identification items keyed to a page). Copy three **check questions**, answer them without notes ("don't know" is fine) and rate your confidence (0–100%) for each; do not look at the key yet. If the source has more keyed questions on the same idea, pick three parallel ones for the delayed check. Write your tutor-mode setup: role, task, context (course, level, source; simple English if you want it), constraints (attempt first; one hint at a time; no full answer unless you ask after two attempts; say so if my answer is wrong), output format (one question or hint per turn; ask me to explain it back at the end). Plan to practice on neighboring problems from the same section, not on your check questions. If your source has no keyed questions, write three questions from its headings or learning objectives only and look up the answers after step 3.

## Steps (tool-neutral)
1. **Briefing (5 min).** Review the five steps of a session (Figure 7.11) and the coding rules in section 7.4: the test is "who produced the answer in this turn: you, from your own memory, or the tool?"; a turn is **S** when you produced the answer, when you asked for something that sets up your own attempt, or when the tool gave feedback after your attempt at the same question; it is **C** when you asked for the answer or the tool answered a question you had not attempted; "OK, thanks" turns stay uncoded. Check your setup against Figure 7.10. Privacy: no personal, confidential or patient data; you will remove names and identifiers from the exported transcript.
2. **Dialogue (25 min).** Run your tutor-mode session alone, in a fresh, temporary chat, on neighboring problems, not on your three check questions. Attempt before every hint. Note the turn numbers where you asked for answers. Aim for at least 10 of your own turns.
3. **Closed-book check (10 min).** Devices and notes away. Rate your confidence (0–100%) for each check question, then answer. Answer in the language of your course. Students with testing accommodations get extended time; the step can move to the end of the period.
4. **Score and verify (10 min).** Score your answers against the key. Compute your predicted score (sum of ratings ÷ 100) and your calibration gap (predicted minus actual). A teammate may check your scoring; you may keep your answers private and score them yourself. Check one of the tool's explanations, hints or verdicts against your source, not against the tool's own verdict. If the tool was wrong, or gave the answer despite your setup, **save this for your Failure Diary (Chapter 4).**
5. **Annotate (20 min).** Code at least six turns S or C, each with a reason that names the rule. Each team brings one scaffold turn and one crutch turn to the class discussion.
6. **Exit (5 min).** Write one change you will make before your delayed check.

At home (by the deadline your instructor sets): the delayed closed-book check at least two days after the lab (parallel questions if you have them; rate first, then answer, score and compute the gap), verification of at least two explanations, hints or verdicts, the 200–300-word reflection and the AI Use Declaration (A4). If you miss the lab, run steps 2–5 at home at least two days before the deadline so the delayed check fits, and score yourself.

## Expected observations
Most students ask for an answer at least once, often when tired or stuck, and many tools give away the answer despite the setup at some point. Immediate closed-book scores are usually lower than students predict after a smooth dialogue (a positive calibration gap); delayed scores are often lower again, while predictions get closer. Some students find that the tool marked a wrong attempt as correct; the key catches it. With three questions, results are a signal about the student, not proof of what works.

## If it does not work
If the output does not follow your setup, record that as a result (it belongs in the annotation and possibly the Failure Diary) and restate the constraint once. If a tool refuses or errors, try a smaller concept. If you cannot use a tool, follow the alternative assignment's route with a classmate (peer-tutor script) or by self-explaining a textbook worked example. If you miss the lab, run the same steps at home and score yourself.

## Debrief (3–5)
1. Which turn did the most for your learning, and who produced the answer in it: you, from your own memory, or the tool? (analyze)
2. Where did your prediction differ most from your result, and why? (evaluate)
3. Did the tool ever say a wrong answer was right? How did you find out? (evaluate)
4. What would you change in your setup next time? (create)

## Deliverable and rubric
A4 (Application Assignment, Chapter 7): setup, keyed check questions with first answers, dialogue transcript (on neighboring problems), annotation, closed-book record (immediate and delayed, with predicted scores and gaps), verification, reflection (a)–(e) in 200–300 words, AI Use Declaration. 10-point rubric in the chapter (Task 3, Annotation 1.5, Closed-book record 1.5, Verification 2, Reflection 1.5, Declaration 0.5). Closed-book scores are not graded.

## Accessibility and alternatives
No-device or opt-out route: the alternative assignment (human or text scaffold plus annotation of the dated transcript pair). Screen-reader-friendly record sheet (a plain table). Extended time for closed-book steps. The dialogue may be in any language, with English glosses of annotated turns. Self-scoring keeps answers private.

## Safety and privacy
No personal, confidential or patient data in prompts. Use a fresh, temporary chat where the tool allows it. Remove names and identifiers from exported transcripts before submitting. Marcus's rule applies to everyone in clinical programs: no patient information in any AI tool.

## Instructor notes
Post on the course site before the lab: the dated answer-mode/tutor-mode transcript pair for one concept and the peer-tutor question script. Remind students that scores are not graded, so there is no reason to look anything up during the closed-book step, and that check questions stay out of the dialogue (otherwise the check measures memory of the dialogue). Collect anonymized scaffold/crutch turns for a review in a later concept session.
