# Case C30: Three texts on machine understanding, and a 2026 test (1950–2026)

## Context
Whether a machine can understand language is one of the oldest questions about AI. Three texts frame it: Turing's proposal to replace "Can machines think?" with the imitation game (1950); Searle's Chinese Room argument against the view that running the right program is enough for understanding (1980); and Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and Shmitchell's "stochastic parrots" paper, which describes language models as producing linguistic form without reference to meaning (2021). In 2026 a peer-reviewed study reported that persona-prompted language models pass a standard three-party Turing test (Jones & Bergen, 2026).

## Timeline
- **1950** — Turing, "Computing machinery and intelligence," *Mind*: proposes the imitation game; answers nine objections; predicts that in about fifty years an average interrogator would have at most a 70% chance of making the right identification after five minutes (confirmed: section 6, read in a course copy at fact-check).
- **1966** — Weizenbaum describes ELIZA (confirmed).
- **1980** — Searle, "Minds, brains, and programs," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*: the Chinese Room; the systems, robot and brain simulator replies and his answers; he says the robot reply concedes that form alone is not enough and adds causal links to the world (confirmed; read in a course-hosted copy of the published pages at fact-check).
- **2020** — Bender & Koller, ACL: form alone cannot yield meaning (the octopus) (confirmed).
- **2021** — Bender et al., FAccT: "stochastic parrots"; risks of ever-larger models (confirmed).
- **March 2025** — Jones & Bergen post the preprint (arXiv 2503.23674) (confirmed).
- **May 19, 2026** — Jones & Bergen, *PNAS* 123(21), e2524472123 (confirmed; full text read via Europe PMC, 2026-10-18).

## What the AI did
In the 2026 studies (five-minute, three-party games; two randomized, preregistered studies on independent populations), GPT-4.5 prompted to adopt a humanlike persona (a young, introverted person who uses slang) was judged to be the human 73% of the time (185 of 254 games), significantly more often than the real human; LLaMa-3.1-405B with the same prompt, 56%; without the persona prompt, GPT-4.5 36% and LLaMa-3.1-405B 38% (full text and Table 1; the abstract lists the pair as "38% and 36%" without naming the models); baselines ELIZA 23% and GPT-4o 21%. A third study with 15-minute games: LLaMa-3.1 56% and GPT-5 59%, both persona-prompted. Interrogators' reasons focused on style and social-emotional cues (confirmed: abstract and full text). Authors: Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen. No output was shown to be wrong; the systems did what the study asked.

## What humans did
The authors concluded that the results constitute empirical evidence that artificial systems can pass a standard three-party Turing test, and framed the test as a measure of how humans distinguish other humans from machines (confirmed). In this book's reading, the result did not move the philosophical debate: each side could read it as support (inferred: the book's reading; no survey of opinion change was found).

## Consequences (documented only)
None in the sense of harm. The texts shaped a debate that the 2026 result did not settle: each side can read it as support (inferred: the book's reading).

| Text | It claimed | It did not claim |
|---|---|---|
| Turing (1950) | The game could replace the question; objections fail; a fifty-year prediction | That the machines of his day think; he treated sustained, satisfactory answers as the evidence to use, not as a proof |
| Searle (1980) | Running the right program is not by itself enough for understanding | That machines can never think |
| Bender et al. (2021) | Language models stitch together form without reference to meaning; scale brings costs and risks | That language models are useless |
| Jones & Bergen (2026) | Persona-prompted models pass a standard three-party test; judges relied on style and social cues | That the models understand |
