## Context
The Targeted Compliance Framework is the set of rules under which the Australian government can suspend or reduce welfare payments when people do not meet "mutual obligation" requirements. The department said it had identified examples where the IT system operating the framework did not operate in line with the law and policies, including in ways that affected people's payments, and that the government commissioned an independent assurance review of that system (confirmed: DEWR, 2025a). Deloitte undertook the review from December 2024 to June 2025 (confirmed: DEWR, 2026). The review covered the department's IT system, not partner agencies' systems or human decision-making (confirmed: DEWR, 2025a). Contract value about A$440,000, about US$290,000 (reported: Associated Press, 2025) [VERIFY AusTender].

## Timeline
- December 2024 – June 2025: review conducted (confirmed).
- August 14, 2025: the department publishes the final report and statement of assurance, with more than 200 pages of detailed assessments of business rules (confirmed: DEWR, 2025a). The Associated Press wrote that the report was "first published" in July; the department's own statement gives August 14, 2025 (the report's own date [VERIFY on the original PDF]).

- October 2025: the department says Deloitte agreed to repay the final instalment under its contract (reported: Associated Press, 2025); a department spokesperson later confirms a refund of just over A$97,000 (reported: CFO Dive, 2025); A$97,587 per a Senate Estimates answer as relayed by the Australian Greens [VERIFY Hansard].

## What the AI did
The corrected report disclosed that a generative AI system (Azure OpenAI) was used in its preparation (reported: Associated Press, 2025; other outlets quote "a generative AI large language model (Azure OpenAI GPT-4o) based tool chain") [VERIFY wording and page in the corrected report]. No public source says which parts of the report the system produced. That it produced the incorrect references is **inferred**.

## What humans did
Before publication: two checkpoints existed, the firm's own review of its report and the department's acceptance and publication of it; what either checked is not on the public record (inferred). After publication: Rudge reported up to 20 errors, including a book attributed to Professor Lisa Burton Crawford that does not exist and a quotation attributed to a Federal Court judgment that the reporting described as made up; he said colleagues' work was cited as "tokens of legitimacy" (reported: Associated Press, 2025). Deloitte confirmed some footnotes and references were incorrect (confirmed via the department: DEWR, 2025b) and told AP the matter had been "resolved directly with the client" (reported). The department said the report's substance and recommendations were unchanged (reported: Associated Press, 2025; consistent with DEWR, 2025b, "continues to focus efforts on addressing the substance and recommendations"). Senator Barbara Pocock (Australian Greens) said the full fee should be repaid (reported: Associated Press, 2025).

## A better workflow (AI Work Loop, for the firm and the client)
Frame: decide which parts of a report an AI tool may draft and which require a named expert. Verify: open every reference and check that it exists and says what is claimed (Chapter 8's checks), and trace every legal quotation to the judgment itself (Chapter 9, 9.4). Decide: the client accepts the report only with a reference check on record. Disclose: state AI use where readers will see it (Chapter 10 discusses whether a methodology note is enough).
