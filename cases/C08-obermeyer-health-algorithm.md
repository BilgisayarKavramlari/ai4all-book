# Case: A Health Algorithm That Used Cost as a Proxy for Need (Obermeyer et al., 2019)

Chapter: 11 (pointer from 16) · Field: health · Failure label: bias (primary, **documented**: leaf "skewed recommendations"; the article measured lower scores for Black patients at equal health) · chain links 1 and 3 documented; link 2 "checked before use?" failed, **inferred** (the review's effect is the authors' simulation); link 4 "loss or harm" **inferred** (an estimate from the authors' simulation) · Outcome: negative (biased targeting; regulators' inquiry; a revised label tested)

Facts are stated in three attribution tiers (book-wide rule): **confirmed** = the published article (Obermeyer et al., 2019; full text read 2026-10-18) or the New York regulators' letter (read 2026-10-18); **reported** = a named outlet; **inferred** = this book's reading.

## Context
Health systems and insurers use commercial algorithms to choose patients for "high-risk care management" programs, which add resources such as dedicated nurses and extra primary care appointments (confirmed: article). The algorithm studied is, in the authors' words, one of the largest and most typical examples of a class of commercial risk-prediction tools that, by industry estimates, are applied to roughly 200 million people in the United States each year (confirmed: article; the 200 million figure is the industry's estimate for the class, not for this product). It predicts total medical expenditure in the following year from the previous year's insurance claims (demographics such as age and sex, insurance type, diagnosis and procedure codes, medications, detailed costs) and specifically excludes race (confirmed: article).

## Timeline
- 2013–2015: all primary care patients enrolled in risk-based contracts at a large academic hospital; main sample 6,079 self-identified Black and 43,539 White patients, observed over 11,929 and 88,080 patient-years (confirmed: article).
- In the health system studied: scores above the 97th percentile automatically identify patients for program enrollment; above the 55th, patients are referred to their primary care doctor, who is prompted to consider enrollment; the article calls the algorithm a screening tool not used for enrollment decisions in isolation (confirmed: article).
- Analysis: done with no contact with the manufacturer; no compensation, no confidentiality agreements (confirmed: article's competing-interests statement).
- Before publication: the authors contacted the manufacturer, which replicated the analysis on its national dataset of 3,695,943 commercially insured patients (by one measure, Black patients had 48,772 more active chronic conditions than White patients at the same score); an index combining health and cost predictions reduced the number of excess active chronic conditions to 7,758, an 84% reduction by that measure (confirmed as what the authors report).
- October 24, 2019: article released online; October 25, 2019: issue date of *Science* 366(6464) (confirmed: article record; Ledford, 2019).
- October 25, 2019: New York's Department of Financial Services and Department of Health write to UnitedHealth Group's chief executive about "Impact Pro", which they describe as Optum's data analytics program, citing the study; they ask the company to investigate and to stop using any program it cannot show does not rely on racial biases or produce racially disparate impacts (confirmed: the letter).
- After: as of October 2026, no public update on the New York inquiry or on changes to the product has been found (a 2023 shareholder proxy-statement filing with the SEC mentions that the New York departments opened an investigation and reports no outcome; per the fact-check). What changed is unknown (inferred).

## What the AI did
It produced risk scores that, at the same score, corresponded to worse health for Black patients: at the 97th percentile, 4.8 vs. 3.8 chronic conditions (26.3% more) (confirmed). In the authors' simulation, removing this gap would raise the share of Black patients among those automatically identified from 17.7% to 46.5% (confirmed as a simulation). Mechanism: the label was cost; at a given level of health, Black patients generated on average $1,801 less in costs per year ($1,144 less holding specific illnesses constant), which the authors attribute to two broad channels: barriers to care that fall harder on poorer patients, and direct effects of race such as discrimination by providers and patients' distrust (confirmed: article; the abstract summarizes it as unequal access to care). The algorithm predicted cost well; the authors' own predictors trained on three labels with the same inputs gave Black shares among the top 3% of 14.1% (total cost), 21.0% (avoidable cost) and 26.7% (active chronic conditions) (confirmed: Table 2).

## What humans did
- The industry judged such algorithms by cost prediction: the article notes that a Society of Actuaries evaluation of the ten most widely used algorithms, including this one, used cost prediction as its accuracy metric (confirmed as stated in the article).
- Doctors reviewed patients referred by the score. Enrolled patients were 19.2% Black (11.9% in the whole sample). The authors' simulations on observable factors only: random enrollment within score bins 18.3% (P = 0.83 against observed); selecting the most predicted active chronic conditions within bins 26.9%; enrolling by highest predicted cost 17.2%; by most predicted active chronic conditions 29.2%. The authors conclude doctors redress "a small part" of the bias, far less than a differently labeled algorithm (confirmed: article).
- The article does not name the product. The regulators' letter calls it Impact Pro, Optum's data analytics program (confirmed as the regulators' description); news reports named Optum as the maker (reported: Ledford, 2019, *Nature*; Johnson, 2019, *Washington Post*). Optum said it appreciated the researchers' work but considered the study's conclusion misleading, because the cost model is one of many data elements used to select patients (reported: Ledford, 2019). NBC News (November 6, 2019) quoted Optum appreciating the researchers' validation that the cost model within Impact Pro was highly predictive of cost (reported).

## Failure label (Signature 5 and the failure-to-harm chain; figures F11.19 and F11.20)
- **Tree — bias, leaf "skewed recommendations", documented (solid).** The article measures the gap in scores at equal health. This is an output failure of a prediction system, so it goes on the tree.
- **Chain — link 1 "The output is wrong" documented; link 2 "Checked before use?" failed, inferred (dashed)**: evaluation by cost accuracy is documented, but how much doctors' review corrected is the authors' simulation on observable factors, which excludes unobserved administrative and human factors; **link 3 "Someone relies on it" documented** (patients above the 97th percentile flagged by the score alone); **link 4 "Loss or harm" inferred (dashed)**: fewer Black patients identified for extra help than if the score had reflected health is an estimate from a simulation; there is no public record of harm to individual patients.
- **Human side, on link 2:** doctors saw only patients the score referred (≥ 55th percentile), so sicker patients scored lower were never reviewed: a documented feature of the design. Automation bias is this book's reading of why doctors corrected little; the authors report only the size of the correction ("a small part") and do not give a cause (inferred).
- **No overconfidence label:** no evidence about expressed certainty.

## Consequences (documented only)
Biased targeting measured in one hospital and replicated by the manufacturer on national data (as the authors report); a regulators' letter (confirmed); a combined label tested with an 84% reduction by one measure, in the manufacturer's data (authors' report). No court finding is reported here.

## A better workflow (AI Work Loop, for the team that builds or buys such a score)
Frame: state the goal as health need and choose a label that measures it (or several). Verify: before use, compare scores with health measures group by group, not only with costs. Decide: review thresholds with people who can override, and record overrides; check a sample of patients below the referral threshold; decide which scores may trigger action without review. Disclose: tell the hospitals using the score what it predicts and what it does not.
